# Restarting Your Quiz, Live Music, and Sport Nights for Maximum ROI

Entertainment nights either anchor your week or quietly eat your margin. The difference is almost never the act, the questions or the fixture. It is structure.

Most relaunches fail for the same three reasons: no calendar, so people cannot form a habit; no booking system, so you cannot staff it; and no numbers, so you never find out which night is carrying the other two.

I run The Anchor in Stanwell Moor and I have rebuilt all three of these formats more than once, usually after letting one drift.

Fix those three and the rest is detail. Here is how to bring back quiz nights, [live music that pays its way](/licensees-guide/live-music-events-for-pubs), and sport with something better than hope.

## Step 1: Build an 8-week calendar

Eight weeks is long enough to build a habit and short enough that you will actually finish it. Map it out before you book anything, using the same planning rhythm as our guide to [running successful pub events](/licensees-guide/how-to-run-successful-pub-events).

| Week | Format | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Quiz | Rebuild the midweek habit |
| 2 | Live music | Pull a different crowd in |
| 3 | Sport screening | Use a fixture you already know about |
| 4 | Quiz with a twist | Give regulars a reason to bring friends |
| 5 | Quiz | Normal service, protect the habit |
| 6 | Live music | Rebook the act that worked |
| 7 | Sport plus food bundle | Test spend per head |
| 8 | Quiz finals night | Reward the league, sell the next eight weeks |

Three rules for choosing days. Check what the pubs within two miles already run, because being second-best quiz on a Wednesday is a waste of a night. Check the school calendar, because half term flattens family and quiz trade. And check the fixture list before you commit a music night to a date when everyone is watching football.

Then the hard part: never cancel. A quiz that runs 47 weeks a year beats a better quiz that runs when you feel like it. If you cannot commit to weekly, run it fortnightly and say so.

## Step 2: Get the licensing straight before you book anyone

Do this in the first week, because it changes what you can afford.

**Live music.** The performance itself usually needs no extra entertainment licensing. Amplified live music between 8am and 11pm in premises licensed to sell alcohol for drinking on site is exempt for audiences up to 500, and unamplified music in those hours has no audience limit. Push past 11pm and your premises licence conditions apply again.

**The music rights are separate.** Live sets, recorded playlists, background music and a TV in the corner all sit under TheMusicLicence from PPL PRS, priced on how you use music and the size of your venue. There is more detail in our guide to [what PRS and PPL actually cost a pub](/licensees-guide/prs-ppl-music-licensing-pubs). Get a quote before you build a music programme, not after.

**Sport.** Commercial subscriptions from Sky and TNT are priced against your rateable value, so two pubs on the same street can pay very different amounts. Get your own quote rather than trusting the number your mate down the road quotes you. Showing sport on a domestic subscription is not a grey area, it is illegal, and it gets pubs prosecuted every season.

**Quiz prizes.** A general knowledge quiz is a game of skill, so it sits outside the gaming rules that catch cards and bingo. A raffle on the night is different: that is a lottery. If you run one, it has to be incidental to the event, tickets sold and drawn there and then, and the proceeds must go to a purpose other than your own profit, normally a charity. Prize costs taken from ticket sales are capped at £500 with up to £100 of expenses, and you cannot roll a prize over to next week.

## Step 3: Pre-book and pre-pay

Walk-ins are lovely and impossible to staff for. Get commitment in advance.

Take a small deposit on premium tables and on anything with food attached: £5 a head, redeemable against the bill, is enough to change behaviour without feeling grabby. We cut booking no-shows by 89% at The Anchor by taking deposits and confirming properly, and the second half of that sentence matters as much as the first.

The mechanics that do the work:

- A booking link that works on a phone in two taps. A phone list in a folder works too, but it must be one place, not three.
- A confirmation the moment they book, restating date, time, table and numbers.
- A reminder the day before with a one-tap way to cancel. People who can cancel easily do, which frees the table for someone else.
- A named cut-off for releasing unclaimed tables, usually fifteen minutes after the start.

## Step 4: Tier the experience

Give people a ladder to climb rather than one price to accept.

- **Standard:** entry and a seat, no reservation.
- **Plus:** a reserved table, a snack board on arrival, priority on the quiz sheets.
- **VIP:** best table in the room, welcome drinks, a host check-in during the interval.

Price the middle tier as the obvious choice. If standard entry is £3 a head and Plus is £8 with a board that costs you £2.50 in product, the Plus table is better value for the guest and considerably better for you. Cap VIP at two or three tables so it stays genuinely scarce.

## Step 5: Script the hosts

A scripted host earns more than a charming one, and a charming host with a script earns most of all.

Give quizmasters and MCs a printed running order with the sell built in: what to say at 8:00, what to announce before the interval, what to say when handing out answer sheets. Specific lines beat vague instructions, and the same principles apply as in our [upselling scripts for bar teams](/licensees-guide/upselling-secrets-training-scripts).

Four cues that pay for themselves:

1. **Interval push.** "Kitchen closes in twenty minutes, quiz platters are at the bar."
2. **Round sponsor.** "Round four is brought to you by the sharing jug, two for one on the second one before nine."
3. **Next date.** Announced twice, once mid-quiz and once at the results.
4. **The rebook.** "Tables for next week are open at the bar now."

If you are rebuilding the format itself as well as the commercial side, revisit the [quiz night format basics](/licensees-guide/quiz-night-101) before you write the first round.

## Step 6: Maximise food and drink

Bundles, not discounts. A discount lowers your take on people who were coming anyway. A bundle raises spend per head.

- Quiz fuel platter for a table of four, priced so the table thinks "that's easy" and you hold your normal food margin.
- Matchday pitcher and a basket, sold at the whistle rather than at the door.
- A tasting board for music nights, three of something with a card explaining what they are.

Keep the kitchen offer to five items maximum on event nights. Two chefs and a full room cannot do the whole menu, and slow food is what turns a good night into a bad review.

**Worked example.** A solo act costs you £200. If your wet gross profit is 60%, you need £333 of extra net drink sales to cover the fee, and net sales of £333 means £400 through the till once the VAT goes back on. At around £14 a head that is roughly 29 extra people through the door. That is your break-even headcount, and it is the only number you need before you say yes to an act. Run the same sum for every fee you are quoted.

## Step 7: Capture data and follow up

Collect a name and a contact at the point of booking, with a clear tick box, and use it.

Send a thank you within 48 hours that contains exactly one call to action: book next week. Then a reminder on the morning of the next event. That is two messages, not a newsletter.

Keep a simple league table for quiz teams. People come back for standings far more reliably than they come back for prizes, and a league gives you a legitimate reason to message them every week.

## Step 8: Measure the ROI

One page per night, filled in the next morning while it is fresh.

| Line | Where it comes from |
|---|---|
| Wet sales | Till report for the session |
| Dry sales | Till report for the session |
| Product cost | Sales less your usual gross profit percentage |
| Host or act fee | Invoice |
| Extra staff hours | Rota, at true cost including National Insurance and holiday pay |
| Prize cost | Product cost of a bar tab, not its face value |
| Contribution | What is left |

Then compare it with a baseline: the same night of the week when you run nothing. If a quiz adds £120 of contribution over a blank Tuesday and the quizmaster costs £60, it is working but it is thin, and the fix is spend per head rather than more marketing.

Review the eight weeks together at the end. Drop the weakest format, repeat the strongest, and rebook the acts and hosts who actually filled the room.

## Common mistakes

- **Running too many formats at once.** Three new nights in one week means three half-promoted nights.
- **Cancelling when it is quiet.** One cancellation costs you a month of habit.
- **Letting guests walk in without a booking plan.** You cannot staff or stock what you cannot see coming.
- **Paying for sport you do not sell.** A subscription is a fixed cost whether you show twelve matches a month or two, so work out your cost per match shown.
- **Judging a night on takings alone.** Takings are not contribution, and the fee comes out of the second one.
- **Forgetting to promote the next night during the current one.** Your best audience is already in the room.

## Quick checklist

- 8-week calendar set, clashes and half term checked.
- TheMusicLicence quoted, sport contract understood, raffle rules checked.
- Booking system live with deposits on premium tables.
- Three tiers priced, middle tier built to be the obvious choice.
- Host script printed with four selling cues.
- Event menu cut to five items and bundled.
- Follow-up message written before week one.
- Per-night profit and loss template ready.

## Mini FAQ

**Should I run all three formats every week?**
No. Rotate them so each feels like an occasion and each has room to grow its own crowd.

**Do deposits scare people off?**
Small redeemable deposits usually increase commitment without reducing demand. The people they deter are the ones who were not coming anyway.

**What if the first few weeks are quiet?**
Expect that, and budget for it. Give it a couple of months before you judge it, and change one variable at a time rather than everything at once.
