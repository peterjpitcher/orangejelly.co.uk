# Party and Event Food: The Allergen and Safety Rules for Pubs

This guide is for licensees who take party bookings: birthdays, christenings, wakes, weddings, work parties, buffets and set-menu functions. A party is not normal service. One person orders for many, often weeks ahead, the food goes out on a buffet or all at once, and extra hands join the shift. A kitchen that copes well every day can still be caught out.

Event food in a pub is not home cooking. Even when the buffet is simple, or bought in from a shop, you are supplying food to the public from a commercial kitchen, and the same rules apply. Sourcing, preparation, safe holding and allergen information all deserve the attention you give your everyday menu. The rules exist to protect your guests, and they are also what let a pub run functions confidently, at pace and at scale. So this guide explains the reason behind each precaution, not only the precaution itself.

The guide separates law from advice throughout. Where something is a legal requirement, it says so. Where it is Food Standards Agency advice, or our own recommendation, it says that instead. This guide is general information, not legal advice. For the full rules, or whenever you are unsure, read the official guidance linked throughout and listed at the end, or ask your council's food safety team.

Food Standards Agency guidance covers England, Wales and Northern Ireland. Scotland has its own regulator, Food Standards Scotland, and some of its rules differ. Where they do, this guide says so.

It is about events. For the allergen system you run every day, and for data protection, see [food allergies and data protection](/guides/food-allergies-gdpr-compliance). For planning and running the event itself, see [how to run successful pub events](/guides/how-to-run-successful-pub-events). It is written by a working licensee: at The Anchor, our own venue, private hire bookings have grown by 567%.

## Why a party catches out a well-run kitchen

Four things make a party different from a normal service:

1. **One person speaks for many.** The organiser books, chooses and pays, and may not know what every guest can eat.
2. **Orders arrive weeks ahead.** A pre-order taken by email may be served a month or more later, after a supplier has changed a product or a recipe has moved on.
3. **Food is batch-cooked and displayed.** Large quantities, cooked in advance and set out on a buffet, need different controls from a plate cooked to order.
4. **Extra hands join the shift.** Casual staff, family helpers and agency staff may not know the menu, the allergen chart or the routine.

The numbers are not small. The Food Standards Agency puts the number of UK adults with a food allergy at around 6%, and says about 1 person in 100 has coeliac disease.

One inquest shows how this happens. In February 2023 a 24-year-old woman died after a family lunch at a Dorset pub. The meal had been ordered in advance by email, and the pub had not been told of any allergy. The coroner recorded an accidental death, and the inquest heard that the council took no action because the pub had complied with all requirements. Her father said the family did not blame the pub, and suggested that more prompts about allergens at booking and ordering could prevent it happening again.

The pub had broken no rule. Following the minimum did not prevent it, which is why this guide gives the advice as well as the law.

## What is law and what is advice

The law sets the minimum: you must be able to tell guests which of the 14 allergens are in what you serve, and that information must be accurate. As of October 2026 no UK nation requires allergen information for non-prepacked food to be in writing. The Food Standards Agency's 2025 guidance states that the minimum legal requirement has not changed.

The agency's best practice goes further. Published in February 2025, it is to make allergen information easily available in writing and to back it with a conversation. The agency states that businesses are not required by law to follow it.

The table sets out which is which, and where each applies:

| What you do | Status | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Tell guests which of the 14 allergens are in every food and drink you provide, free items included | Law | UK |
| Make sure that information is accurate | Law | UK |
| If staff give it in conversation, display a notice telling guests to ask | Law | UK |
| Give it in writing as well as in conversation | Food Standards Agency best practice, not law | England, Wales, Northern Ireland |
| For food sold by phone or online, give it before the purchase is concluded and again at delivery | Law | UK |
| For a pre-ordered group booking, discuss it with each guest on the day, before food is served | Food Standards Agency best practice | England, Wales, Northern Ireland |
| On a buffet, give it for each dish separately | Food Standards Agency guidance | England, Wales, Northern Ireland |
| Label food you pack before the guest chooses it with its name and a full ingredients list, allergens emphasised | Law, since 1 October 2021 | UK |
| Keep chilled food at 8°C or below | Law | England, Wales, Northern Ireland. Recommended in Scotland |
| Keep hot food for service at 63°C or above | Law | England, Wales, Northern Ireland. Above 63°C in Scotland |
| Use one display period of under four hours for cold food, or under two hours for hot food, once only | Law | England, Wales, Northern Ireland only |
| Reheat food to at least 82°C | Law | Scotland only |
| Review your food safety procedures when a product, process or step changes | Law | UK |
| Keep your council's information about your business up to date | Law | UK |
| Supervise, instruct or train everyone who handles food | Law | UK |

## Before you confirm the booking

**Ask every guest, not only the organiser.** The Food Standards Agency says businesses should ask all customers whether they have any allergen requirements. The agency suggests two ways to ask: whether anyone has "a food allergy, intolerance or coeliac disease", or whether anyone has "any allergen requirements". At a party, that question has to reach past the organiser to every guest. Put it on the booking form, in writing:

> **Allergies and dietary needs**
>
> 1. Does anyone in your party have a food allergy, intolerance or coeliac disease? Yes or no.
> 2. If yes, for each guest: their name, what they must avoid, and anything else we should know.
> 3. Who should we speak to on the day?
>
> We will confirm allergen information with each of these guests on the day, before any food is served. Please tell us at once if anything changes.

**Pre-orders.** Where non-prepacked food is sold at a distance, for example ordered by telephone or online, the law says allergen information must be available to the customer, free of charge, before the purchase is concluded and again at the moment of delivery. Selling at a distance means the sale is agreed without the business and the customer being present together.

The rule for food ordered by phone or online is clear. Whether a party pre-order falls under it has not been spelled out by any regulator, so do what satisfies both readings: give the allergen information in writing with the menu or the pre-order form, and go through it again with each guest on the day.

That also follows the agency's advice. Food ordered before an event by one person or organisation on behalf of others is non-prepacked food, and the Food Standards Agency says allergen information should be provided to anyone eating it, in conversation or in writing. For a pre-ordered group booking, the agency says allergen information should also be discussed with individuals on the day, before the food is served, because ingredients could have changed or details been missed in the original order.

**Guests' details.** A named guest's allergy is information about their health, which data protection law treats as special category data. You need a lawful basis and a special category condition to hold it, and the [Information Commissioner's Office](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/lawful-basis/special-category-data/what-are-the-rules-on-special-category-data/) says to collect and keep only the minimum. Decide how long you keep the forms, and write that decision down. The guide to [food allergies and data protection](/guides/food-allergies-gdpr-compliance) covers this in more detail, and [filling December with party bookings](/guides/pub-christmas-bookings-fill-december) covers the bookings themselves.

## Build the menu and its allergen chart

**One chart for this event's menu.** A food business must be able to tell customers which of the 14 allergens listed in law are in any food or drink it provides that is not prepacked. The information must be accurate, and the food business is responsible for that however the information is produced. For an event, make one chart for that event's menu: every dish, sauce, garnish and canapé, and every drink mixed at the bar, against the 14 allergens:

- celery
- cereals containing gluten, including wheat, rye, barley and oats
- crustaceans, including prawns, crab and lobster
- eggs
- fish
- lupin
- milk
- molluscs, including squid, mussels, cockles, whelks and snails
- mustard
- nuts
- peanuts
- sesame seeds
- soya beans
- sulphur dioxide or sulphites at levels above 10mg per kilogram or per litre

**Fix the recipes.** The Food Standards Agency says to update allergen information whenever recipes or ingredients change, and make sure all staff know. We recommend fixing the event's recipes when the menu is agreed, so the chart that goes out with the pre-order form is the one the kitchen cooks from.

**Check the deliveries.** Check deliveries to make sure everything is what you ordered. If you receive a different product, there may be different allergens in the food, so check any substitution against the chart before it is used.

**Be careful with claims.**

- A "free from" claim is a guarantee that the food is suitable for everyone with that allergy or intolerance, and needs strict controls.
- By law, "gluten-free" may only be used where the food as sold contains no more than 20 mg/kg of gluten. The rule applies to food served in pubs and restaurants.
- The Food Standards Agency advises against statements such as "no gluten containing ingredients", because they can mislead.
- Vegan does not mean free from milk, egg, fish, crustaceans or molluscs. Low-level cross-contamination can occur.
- The agency says "may contain" wording should only be used after a risk assessment, and never alongside a "free from" claim for the same allergen. A blanket "may contain allergens" notice could be deemed misleading.
- "May contain" warnings from your suppliers should be passed on to the customer.

## Drinks count too

The allergen duty covers drinks as well as food. It also covers food and drink given away free, such as a welcome drink or complimentary canapés.

Beer made from barley, wheat, rye or oats contains a listed allergen. Spirits distilled from those cereals are exempt from the list, and so is isinglass used to clear beer and wine.

For bottled and canned drinks, the allergens are on the label. For anything mixed at the bar, they belong on your allergen chart: egg white, cream and nut liqueurs are the common ones. We recommend washing shakers and blenders between drinks.

## Buffets: label every dish, control the clock

**Label dish by dish.** On a buffet, allergen information is needed for each food item separately. It should not be provided for the buffet as a whole. That can be done by labelling the allergens in each dish, or by a sign directing customers to ask staff. The information must be visible, clearly legible and easy for the customer to get at.

If staff give the information in conversation and not in writing, the law requires a notice telling customers they can get it by asking staff. The notice must be on a label on the food, or on a notice, menu, ticket or label the customer can easily see where they choose the food.

If cross-contamination cannot be avoided, the Food Standards Agency says to tell customers that you cannot provide an allergen-free dish.

**What we do at The Anchor.** For every finished dish on a buffet, we prepare a readable A4 sheet that sits with the dish. It gives the name of the food, what is in it and its allergens, checked against the recipe and the suppliers' packaging, and a dietary or "free from" claim only where we can stand behind it. Guests can read it while they choose, and a member of staff is there to talk through any individual allergy.

This is how we choose to meet the buffet guidance, not a legal form. The legal minimum is set out above: the information has to be available for each dish, and if staff give it in conversation, a notice must tell guests to ask. A sheet does not replace the conversation with a guest who has an allergy. Food packed before guests choose it needs its own full label under the prepacked for direct sale rules below, and any "free from" claim carries the strict controls described in the menu section.

**Plate allergy guests from the kitchen.** We recommend plating food for a guest with an allergy in the kitchen and not from the buffet, where shared spoons and tongs carry one dish into another.

**Control the clock.** In England, Wales and Northern Ireland the law sets the temperatures and one exception for display:

- Food that needs to be kept cold to stay safe must be kept at 8°C or below. The Food Standards Agency says to set fridges to 5°C or below.
- Cold food may be on display above 8°C for one period of under four hours. This can only be done once.
- Cold food that has been out for less than four hours can go back in the fridge, the agency's guidance says. If it has been out for more than four hours, the same guidance says it must be thrown away.
- Hot food held for service must be kept at 63°C or above.
- Hot food may be kept below 63°C for one period of under two hours. This can only be done once. After that, the agency says, cool it as quickly as possible to 8°C or below, or throw it away.

In England and Wales, the agency's Safer food, better business pack adds two habits for display. Do not top up a dish on display: use up the food that is out before adding new food, and do not mix new hot food with food already on display. And it is a good idea to label food with the time it came out of the fridge.

**Scotland is different.** Scotland's regulations set no 8°C figure and no four-hour or two-hour periods. Food must be kept in a fridge or a cool ventilated place, or above 63°C, and food that is reheated must reach at least 82°C unless that would spoil it. In Scotland, 8°C or below is recommended for chilled food, and Food Standards Scotland's advice for food put out for a party is no more than four hours.

**Food packed in advance.** Food that a business packs before the customer chooses or orders it, and sells from the place it was packed, is called "prepacked for direct sale". Since 1 October 2021 it must carry a label showing the name of the food and a full ingredients list with the allergens emphasised. This is often called Natasha's Law.

At an event, the Food Standards Agency's guidance is that individually packaged items put out for guests to pick are covered, and so are drinks poured and lidded before customers order them. A shared platter is not, and nor is a platter whose cling film is taken off before it is served. Specific food ordered in advance is not prepacked for direct sale, but allergen information must still be provided.

## Plated functions: the right plate to the right guest

At a sit-down function the risk is the wrong plate reaching the wrong guest. The Food Standards Agency's advice:

1. **Pass the order in writing.** Where the person taking the order is not the person preparing the food, the allergen requirement should be passed to the kitchen in writing, with confirmation that it has been received and understood.
2. **Clean down first.** Before preparing a dish for a guest with an allergy, clean work surfaces and equipment thoroughly. Separate boards and equipment are better still.
3. **Start again after a mistake.** If a mistake is made, do not remove the ingredient and serve the dish. Start again from scratch with fresh ingredients. Allergens cannot be removed or destroyed by cooking, and oil that has cooked battered fish cannot be used to cook gluten-free chips.
4. **Make the plate easy to identify.** The finished dish should be easy to identify, for example with a label or a flag.
5. **Confirm it at the table.** The server should confirm it out loud, for example "here's the pasta with no milk".
6. **Nobody guesses.** Staff should not guess or assume what is in a dish.

The agency's allergen checklist asks whether a responsible member of staff is available on each shift to manage requests from customers with allergies. At a function, we recommend naming that person on the team briefing, so everyone knows who to ask.

## Other people's food

**Food you buy in.** Each food business is responsible for the food it supplies and for the accuracy of the allergen information it gives. A business that supplies non-prepacked food to another food business must pass on the information that business needs. If you buy in desserts, canapés or a cake and serve them as part of your own package, get the allergen information from the supplier in writing and put it on the event's chart.

Do not let a familiar name reassure you. Two sausage rolls or two sandwiches from different shops can have different recipes, and one may contain an allergen the other does not. A shop can change a recipe, and a substitute can arrive in place of what you ordered. When you buy in buffet food:

- keep the packaging, or the supplier's ingredient information, for everything you serve;
- record the actual ingredients and allergens of the finished dish, including anything you add to it;
- check any substitution before it goes out;
- make sure the allergen chart and the sheet or label for that dish say the same thing.

The Food Standards Agency's pack suggests asking a supplier three questions: are they registered with the local authority, do they provide allergen information, and do they have a food safety management system.

**A caterer the customer hires.** A caterer supplying food to your guests is a food business in its own right and answers for its own food. No regulator has published a statement on how responsibility divides between the venue and a caterer the customer brings in, so ask your council's food safety team before the event, and remember that whatever your own staff store, reheat or serve is your responsibility.

The Food Standards Agency's guidance is that such a caterer should provide allergen information for its own food. Ask for it in writing before the event, as the checklist below does.

**Food guests bring.** No regulator has published a rule on who holds the allergen duty for food a customer brings in. Decide your house policy and put it in the booking terms: who cuts and serves it, where it is kept, and that you cannot give allergen information for food you did not supply. If you are unsure, ask your council's food safety team.

## Batch cooking, cooling and reheating

Big batches carry their own risk. The bacteria Clostridium perfringens form spores that survive cooking and grow during slow cooling and unrefrigerated storage. The [Food Standards Agency says](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/clostridium-perfringens/clostridium-perfringens) outbreaks tend to happen where large groups of people are being served and keeping food at safe temperatures may be difficult. In England and Wales, its pack advises: avoid cooking large quantities of food in advance unless you need to.

**Cooking.** No UK nation sets a cooking temperature in law. In England, Wales and Northern Ireland, the Food Standards Agency's standard advice is a core temperature of 70°C for 2 minutes, or an equivalent such as 75°C for 30 seconds.

**Cooling.** Across the UK, cooked food that is to be kept must be cooled as quickly as possible. The law sets no time limit.

**Reheating.** In England, Wales and Northern Ireland, the agency's advice is to reheat food until it is steaming hot all the way through, and only reheat it once. In Scotland, food that is reheated must reach at least 82°C unless that would spoil it.

| Control | England, Wales and Northern Ireland | Scotland |
|---|---|---|
| Cold holding | 8°C or below (law). Fridge set to 5°C or below (advice) | No figure in law: a fridge or a cool ventilated place. 8°C or below is recommended |
| Cold food on display | One period of under four hours, once only (law) | No set period in law. Advice: no more than four hours |
| Hot holding | 63°C or above (law) | Above 63°C (law) |
| Hot food on display | One period of under two hours, once only (law) | No set period in law |
| Cooking | No temperature in law. Advice: a core temperature of 70°C for 2 minutes, or 75°C for 30 seconds | No temperature in law. Follow CookSafe |
| Reheating | Advice: steaming hot all the way through, once only | At least 82°C (law), unless that would spoil the food |
| Cooling | As quickly as possible (law). No time limit in law | As quickly as possible (law). No time limit in law |

On Christmas Day 2012 a pub in Hornchurch served lunch to 128 customers. Turkey cooked the day before had not been cooled properly and was not adequately reheated. Thirty-three people fell ill and one woman died. The pub company was fined £1.5 million for placing unsafe food on the market. The chef and the manager were jailed, not for the food poisoning, but for perverting the course of justice: they had filled in false records afterwards.

Records are written at the time, or not at all.

## Barbecues, hog roasts and marquees

Marquees, stalls and other temporary premises have their own legal requirements. Where necessary there must be facilities for washing and drying hands hygienically, an adequate supply of hot or cold drinking water, and arrangements for keeping food at a safe temperature and checking it.

For barbecues in England and Wales, the Food Standards Agency's pack says it is a good idea to cook poultry fully in an oven first and finish it on the barbecue. Never use the same utensils, plates or containers for raw and cooked food.

In England, Wales and Northern Ireland, a business that intends to serve beef burgers less than thoroughly cooked must tell its local authority beforehand and be able to show that suitable controls are in place. The Food Standards Agency says this is a legal requirement because it is a significant change to the business.

An outdoor event may raise licensing questions too, such as a Temporary Event Notice: see the [licensing guide](/guides/pub-licensing-premises-personal-licence-guide). For health and safety beyond food, see the [pub health and safety checklist](/guides/pub-health-safety-checklist).

## Your team on the day

Everyone who handles food must be supervised and instructed or trained in food hygiene to a level that fits the work they do. That includes the extra hands brought in for a party.

In England, Wales and Northern Ireland, the Food Standards Agency says any member of staff who handles food must be trained in food hygiene and safety, including allergens, before they start work. Food handlers do not have to hold a food hygiene certificate, the agency says: what matters is the training itself. The Food Standards Agency offers free online allergen training.

The Food Standards Agency says staff with diarrhoea or vomiting must not return to work until they have had no symptoms for 48 hours. We recommend lining up cover in advance.

Brief the whole team before service, extra staff included. Copy this, fill it in and read it out:

> **Today's event:** [name of party], [number] guests, food from [time].
>
> **Allergy guests:** [name, table, what they must avoid].
>
> **The allergen chart** for today's menu is [where it is kept].
>
> **If a guest asks what is in a dish:** check the chart. If it is not on the chart, ask [named person]. Nobody guesses.
>
> **Allergy meals** leave the kitchen separately, labelled with the guest's name. [Named person] takes each one to the table and confirms it with the guest.
>
> **If a guest is having a reaction:** call 999 and say you think it is an anaphylactic reaction. Tell [named person]. Send someone outside to meet the ambulance.

## Paperwork: what to update and who to tell

Every food business must have written food safety procedures based on HACCP principles (Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points), and must review them whenever a product, a process or any step changes. A first buffet, hog roast or batch-cooked set menu may be exactly that kind of change.

The standard packs are Safer food, better business in England and Wales, Safe Catering in Northern Ireland and CookSafe in Scotland. Check that yours covers everything the event involves.

A food business must keep its council's information about it up to date, including any significant change in activities. The Food Standards Agency says you should tell your local authority about changes to the food activities you are registered for, and about changes that may add risk.

The law does not list what counts as a significant change, and no official source names buffets, barbecues, hog roasts or outside catering. If events mean a kind of food activity you were not doing before, tell your council's food safety team in writing and let them decide.

BII members have a Health, Safety and Fire Compliance helpline, which the BII describes as covering queries such as correspondence with Environmental Health Officers and food standards. The number is on the [BII's helplines page](https://bii.org/resources-hub/helplines/). For inspections and ratings, see the [guide to a five-star food hygiene rating](/guides/food-hygiene-rating-five-star-guide).

## If something goes wrong

**If a guest has a reaction.** The Food Standards Agency's Safer food, better business pack and the [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/anaphylaxis/) give these steps:

1. Ring 999 straight away and ask for an ambulance with a paramedic. Say you think it is an anaphylactic reaction.
2. If the guest has an adrenaline auto-injector, help them to get it and use it.
3. Do not move them. The NHS says the person should lie down, with legs raised, or sit up slowly if struggling to breathe, and should not stand or walk at any time, even if they feel better.
4. The NHS says to give a second injector after 5 minutes if there is no improvement.
5. Send someone outside to wait for the ambulance.

**Afterwards.**

- Leave the records exactly as they were written at the time. Do not tidy, complete or rewrite them.
- If you believe food you have supplied may be harmful, you must tell the authorities immediately. For a single pub that means the council's food safety team, which you can [find on GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/find-your-local-food-safety-team).
- We recommend telling your insurer promptly as well. See the [pub insurance guide](/guides/pub-insurance-cover-guide).

## Event-day checklist

Print this and keep it with each booking.

**When the booking comes in**

- [ ] Allergy question asked in writing, and answered for every guest, not only the organiser
- [ ] Menu or pre-order form sent with allergen information for every dish and drink on it
- [ ] A named contact for the party recorded
- [ ] Any outside caterer or cake maker asked for allergen information in writing

**The week before**

- [ ] Allergen chart made for this event's menu: every dish, sauce, garnish, canapé and bar-mixed drink
- [ ] Each allergy guest's meal agreed with the kitchen, in writing
- [ ] Recipes fixed, with any change going back through the chart
- [ ] Packaging or supplier ingredient information kept for every bought-in item
- [ ] Food safety pack checked: does it cover everything this event involves?

**On the day, before service**

- [ ] Deliveries checked against the order, and any substitution checked against the chart
- [ ] Allergen information for each buffet dish prepared or updated (at The Anchor, an A4 sheet per finished dish), checked against the recipe and the packaging, and placed where guests can read it while they choose
- [ ] Notice on display telling guests to ask about allergens
- [ ] Whole team briefed, extra staff included: check the chart and the sheets, never guess
- [ ] Time written down for anything going out on display

**During service**

- [ ] Allergen requirements confirmed with each guest before food is served
- [ ] Allergy meals plated separately, labelled with the guest's name and carried by one named person
- [ ] Server says at the table what the dish is free from
- [ ] Display times kept: in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, under four hours for cold food and under two hours for hot food, once only
- [ ] Nothing topped up: a fresh dish replaces an empty one
- [ ] Chart and buffet information updated together after any last-minute change

**Afterwards**

- [ ] Menu, chart, pre-orders and briefing sheet filed together
- [ ] Records left exactly as they were written at the time
- [ ] A decision made, and written down, on how long guests' allergy details are kept
