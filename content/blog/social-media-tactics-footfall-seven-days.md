# Social Media Tactics That Drive Footfall in Seven Days

A single pretty post rarely changes trade. A short, tight sprint can. The goal is to fill specific seats on specific nights, not to chase likes.

Most licensees post because they feel they ought to. There is no target, no deadline and no way of telling whether it worked. Trade stays flat and social media gets the blame, when the real problem is that nobody was ever asked to do anything.

This seven-day plan gives you a repeatable burst that turns social posts into bookings, and it sits neatly on top of your [everyday social media strategy](/guides/social-media-strategy-for-pubs). One offer, one week, one number you are trying to hit.

## Pick the sprint target before you pick the posts

Choose the night that has genuine spare capacity and decent margin. Not your busiest night, because you cannot serve more people than you already do, and not your absolute deadest night either, because the gap is too big to close in a week.

Write down three things: the night, the current covers, and the target covers. That single line governs everything else.

Here is a worked example, and it is only an example, so use your own numbers. Say Thursday steak night seats 40, you currently do 14 covers, and you set a target of 32. That is 18 extra covers. At £28 a head that is £504 more through the till. Strip the VAT out and it is £420 of net sales, so at a 65% blended gross profit you keep roughly £273. A £30 ad spend against £273 is an easy decision. A £30 ad spend against a night you cannot describe in numbers is a guess.

At The Anchor, this kind of tight, repeatable push around specific nights is a large part of how we increased table bookings by 403%. The mechanism was never clever creative. It was choosing one night at a time and making the ask obvious.

## Day 0: Prep

Block one hour. Do not spread this over the week, because that is how sprints die.

**Pick one event or offer and write it as one sentence.** "Thursday steak night, two 8oz sirloins and a bottle of house red for £45, tables from 6pm." That sentence does not change for seven days. Not the wording, not the price, not the times.

**Set the booking link and the cap.** If you take online bookings, use a link that lands on that specific date rather than your homepage. If you do not, use WhatsApp or Messenger with a pre-written reply your team can send in one tap. If your booking journey takes more than three taps on a phone, fix that before you spend a penny on ads. Plenty of pubs lose bookings at the link rather than at the post, which is worth reading up on if [nobody seems to book tables any more](/guides/nobody-books-tables-anymore).

**Capture ten photos and five short clips.** Shoot vertical, 9:16, on your phone, during a real service if you can, because empty rooms photograph badly. Wipe the table first, shoot the plate you actually serve rather than a styled version, and use a window seat at around 5pm for natural light. Get one clip of a plate landing on the pass, one of the room with people in it, one of a member of staff talking straight to camera about the offer, one of a drink being poured, and one wide shot of the frontage or garden.

## Days 1-2: Proof first

Open with evidence, not enthusiasm. Post the food, the room, the fire, the crowd. Then attach the offer sentence and the link.

The line that works is availability plus deadline: "Thursday steak night, 12 tables left, book now." Availability is honest, specific, and gives people a reason to act today rather than think about it.

Post one Reel or short vertical video and one photo carousel across these two days. Short video reaches people who do not follow you, which is the whole point of a footfall sprint. A still photo mostly reaches people who already know you exist.

Create a Facebook Event for the night as well. It is the most underused free tool in the pub trade: people can mark themselves as going, invite friends, and get an automatic reminder from Meta on the day, which is a reminder you did not have to send.

## Days 3-4: Spotlight guests and staff

People show up for people. Days one and two sold the product. Days three and four sell the room.

Film your chef explaining why the steak is cut the way it is. Film a bar member of staff saying which wine they would pick with it. Post a photo of two regulars at the bar (ask first) with a line about how long they have been coming. Fifteen seconds of a real person beats a polished graphic, and it costs you nothing but the asking.

Keep the call to action identical. Same sentence, same link. It will feel repetitive to you because you have read it forty times. Your followers have seen it perhaps twice.

Use Stories on both days for the smaller stuff: the delivery arriving, the specials board being written, a countdown sticker. Stories carry urgency in a way that feed posts do not. Our [Instagram marketing guide for pubs](/guides/instagram-marketing-for-pubs) covers the day-to-day formats in more detail.

## Day 5: Paid push

Now put money behind the post that already worked. Do not build new creative for the ad. Go into your page insights, find the organic post from days one to four with the highest reach or the most link clicks, and put budget behind that one. The audience has already voted.

Practical settings for a local pub:

| Setting | What to choose | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Radius | 3 to 5 miles around the pub | Your real catchment. A city-wide radius burns budget on people who will never travel. |
| Daily budget | £10 to £20 | Meta needs enough daily volume to optimise. Two days at £15 beats seven days at £4. |
| Duration | 48 hours, ending the night before | Urgency does the work. A fortnight of low-level spend does not. |
| Age range | Match who actually comes | Steak night skews older than a live music night. Do not target 18 to 65 by default. |
| Objective | Traffic or engagement | Pick traffic if the booking link is the goal, engagement if you want messages. |

Even £20 across two days can move bookings when the offer is clear and the radius is tight. If Meta advertising is new to you, work through our guide to [targeted local Facebook ads](/guides/facebook-marketing-local-pubs) first. The two most expensive mistakes are a radius that is too wide and a landing page that is not the booking page.

## Day 6: Partner or swap

Reach you do not pay for is the best reach there is. Ask a [local brand you already work with](/guides/partnering-local-brands-share-marketing) to share your post: the butcher supplying the steak, the brewery, the coffee roaster, the florist next door. Offer to share something of theirs in return so it is a swap rather than a favour.

Also post into the two or three local community Facebook groups that matter in your area. Read the rules first: many ban straight promotion but allow event posts or business posts on a set day. Post as a human being, not as a page with a poster.

Message five local businesses on day six and do not be surprised if only one or two say yes. That is still an audience or two you did not have on day five.

## Day 7: Countdown and close

Post remaining availability in the morning, then again in the afternoon. "Six tables left for tonight" outperforms "come down tonight" because it is a fact rather than a wish.

Reply to every comment and message within the hour today. Someone asking "do you have space at 7?" at 4pm is a booking you lose if you answer at 9pm.

Close the loop after service: post one photo from the night with a thank you and the date of the next one. That photo becomes day zero of your next sprint.

## Asset checklist

- Ten photos: food, drinks, room, crowd, frontage.
- Five short vertical clips of ten to fifteen seconds.
- One simple availability graphic you can edit each day (Canva template is fine).
- One offer sentence, written down, unchanged all week.
- A booking link that works on mobile in three taps or fewer.

## What to track

Keep it to four numbers on a single sheet by the till:

- Booking link clicks (from your booking system or the ad platform).
- Messages and comments asking about availability.
- Actual covers on the night versus the same night last month.
- Total ad spend.

Then ask every guest on the night how they heard about it and tally the answers on a notepad. That tally is unglamorous and it is the most reliable data you will get.

## Common mistakes

- **Changing the message every day.** You get bored long before your audience does. Repetition is the tactic, not a failure of imagination.
- **Posting without a booking link.** No link, no booking.
- **Waiting until the day of the event.** In my experience most people settle a midweek night out two to four days ahead. A post on the afternoon itself catches only the already-free.
- **Spreading a tiny budget over seven days.** £4 a day teaches Meta nothing and reaches nobody.
- **Running the sprint on a night you cannot staff.** Filling 32 covers with a rota built for 14 turns a win into a bad review.

## Make it repeatable

The value of a sprint is not the one night. It is that you now have a format. Same structure, different offer, once or twice a month. By the third run you will shoot the assets faster, you will know which post type earns its ad spend, and you will have a rough cost per cover you can plan against.

Rotate the target night so you are not always propping up the same shift, and keep a one-line log of what each sprint cost and what it returned.

## Quick checklist

- One offer chosen, written as a single sentence.
- Target covers written down next to current covers.
- Assets captured in one session.
- Seven-day schedule planned before day one.
- Booking link tested on a phone.
- Paid boost set for day 5 with a 3 to 5 mile radius.
- Post-night tally of how people heard about it.

## Mini FAQ

**Do I need TikTok for this?**
No. One platform done well beats three done badly. For most UK pubs that platform is Facebook, with Instagram second.

**How long should the sprint run?**
Seven days is enough to build urgency without fatigue. Fourteen days of the same message stops landing.

**What if the night still underperforms?**
Check the funnel in order: did the posts reach people, did people click, did clicks become bookings. Poor reach is a content problem, poor clicks are an offer problem, and poor conversion is a booking-journey problem. Each one has a different fix.
