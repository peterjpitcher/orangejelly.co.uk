# Community Outreach Ideas That Reintroduce Your Pub to the Neighbourhood

When locals forget you exist, no amount of advertising will fix it. Advertising works on people who are already considering you. Reintroduction is a different job, and it is done with presence rather than promotion.

I run The Anchor in Stanwell Moor, and the pattern is always the same. People do not consciously decide to stop coming. The pub simply stops appearing in their week, and a quiet Tuesday becomes a quiet month.

Use this as a three-layer plan: show up, collaborate, then become essential. Each layer takes about a month, and none of it needs a marketing budget.

## Layer 1: Show up where locals already are

Start with low-effort presence. The goal is to make your pub feel part of daily life again before you ask anyone for anything.

- Post a weekly update in local Facebook groups, following [the same rules that make Facebook marketing work for pubs](/licensees-guide/facebook-marketing-local-pubs). Read the group rules first and message the admin before you post anything promotional. Give three genuinely useful posts (a lost dog, a road closure, a local fundraiser) for every one that mentions your pub.
- Offer your space as a free meeting room to one local group, in hours you are not busy. A Tuesday morning from ten until twelve costs you nothing and puts fifteen people in a room that would otherwise be empty. Ask only that they buy their own drinks.
- Put a community noticeboard near the bar and give one person on the team responsibility for clearing it every month. A board of curled, out-of-date flyers tells people you stopped paying attention two years ago.
- Get your events into the channels that already reach the whole village: the parish magazine, the parish council newsletter, the school newsletter and the local paper's what's-on listing. All are free and all reach the people who are not on Instagram.
- Physically attend two community meetings this quarter. The parish council, the residents' association, the local business group. Sit at the back, listen, introduce yourself once.

## Layer 2: Collaborate with local partners

Pick two partners and build a simple shared offer, much like [partnering with local brands to share the marketing effort](/licensees-guide/partnering-local-brands-share-marketing). The right partner has three things: a real audience, a reason to talk to that audience, and a person who actually replies.

| Partner | What they bring | What you offer |
|---|---|---|
| School PTA | A list reaching every family in the catchment | A fundraiser night with a share of takings |
| Sports club | 30 to 100 members, weekly fixtures | A post-match home, a reserved area, a food deal |
| Local charity branch | Volunteers and press contacts | Space, a collection point, a quiz night |
| Makers and small producers | Their own customers and social following | A Sunday afternoon market in a quiet slot |
| Running or walking group | A weekly meeting time you can own | Coffee, water and a warm room afterwards |
| Care home or day centre | Daytime groups with transport already sorted | A quiet lunch service and an accessible route in |

Agree the deal in writing before anything is promoted, even if it is only an email. Cover the date and times, the space they get, what is free and what is charged, who promotes it and where, and what happens if numbers do not turn up. Vague arrangements are where goodwill goes to die.

Two rules on money. If you are running a percentage-of-takings fundraiser, state the percentage and the window clearly ("10% of all food sales between 6pm and 9pm on Thursday"), then hand over a cheque in the pub with a photo. If you want a collection tin on the bar, a static tin on your own premises does not normally need a council permit, because those cover street and house-to-house collections in public places. What you do need is the charity's agreement, their official sealed tin and materials, and a record of what you hand over. Follow the Fundraising Regulator's code and you will be fine.

On raffles, a small raffle run at a single event will usually qualify as an incidental lottery, which needs no licence. The conditions are strict: tickets sold at the venue only and only while the event is running, nothing sold online or in advance, a maximum of £100 of proceeds spent on running it, a maximum of £500 spent on buying prizes, and no rolling the prize over. The draw itself no longer has to happen before everyone goes home, as long as ticket holders can find out the result afterwards. Donated prizes do not count against that £500.

Working with a school adds one more layer. Let them lead on safeguarding, keep alcohol branding off anything that goes home in a book bag, and never put a photo of a child on your socials without written parental consent arranged through the school.

## Layer 3: Become essential to the community

This is where loyalty is created. Make the pub the default place for a specific local need, and repeat it until people stop needing to be reminded.

- A monthly community forum or a councillor question-and-answer evening.
- A yearly volunteer celebration night, hosted free, for the people who run everything else in the village.
- A local history night with photographs and stories from regulars, projected on the wall.
- A warm, free space in winter with tea, a fire and no expectation of a purchase.
- A standing daytime slot for a group that has nowhere else: new parents, a bereavement group, a jobseekers' morning.

Pick one and put it on the same day every month. Rituals create repeat trade in a way that one-off events never will.

Funding exists for this. Pub is The Hub, a not-for-profit that advises licensees on community diversification, runs a Community Services Fund offering grants of up to £6,000 to pubs in rural, remote or deprived areas adding services such as community cafes, shops or activity spaces. A lower ceiling applies in Wales, so check the current terms before you plan around a figure. If your pub is the last one standing in your village, it is worth an expression of interest.

## Turn goodwill into bookings

Outreach is not charity with a bar attached. It should show up in the takings, and the clearest route is private hire.

Every group you host for free is a group that now knows your rooms, your parking and your food. Ask the obvious question at the end: "would this work for your Christmas lunch?" At The Anchor, our own pub, we grew private hire bookings by 567%, and a large share of that came from groups who first walked in for something that had nothing to do with hiring a room.

Do the arithmetic before you agree to anything. As an example, a group of 20 meeting on a Tuesday afternoon and spending an average of £12 a head puts £240 through a till that would otherwise be dead, on staff you are already paying. That is a good trade. The same group asking for exclusive use of your main bar on a Friday night is not.

Rebuilding a lapsed local base takes more than events alone, and our guide on [winning back locals after a slow spell](/licensees-guide/win-back-locals-after-slow-trade) covers the rest of the picture.

## Say yes to this, no to that

Before you agree to anything, answer one question honestly: if this went perfectly, would the same people be back next month without being asked?

If the answer is yes, run it. If it only produces one good night and a photo for the noticeboard, either turn it into something that repeats or turn it down. A charity night that costs you a full team, a closed bar and a Saturday does less for the community than a monthly quiz that quietly raises money for twelve months, because the quiz is still there in December.

Two practical limits sit under that question. It has to run on a normal rota, without anybody working a favour they will resent by March. And it has to happen in hours you are not already selling. Generosity on a Tuesday morning is generosity. Generosity on a Friday night is a hole in your week.

## Example 90-day outreach plan

**Month 1: show up.** Week one, rebuild the noticeboard and message two Facebook group admins. Week two, offer your space free to one group. Week three, get listed in the parish magazine and the school newsletter. Week four, attend one community meeting.

**Month 2: collaborate.** Shortlist four partners, contact all four, expect two to reply. Agree one co-branded event in writing and set a date at least six weeks out. If you want an easy first fundraiser, the [Macmillan Coffee Morning](/licensees-guide/macmillan-coffee-morning-pub-guide), traditionally held on the last Friday in September, is about as low-risk as it gets.

**Month 3: become essential.** Launch one repeating monthly ritual, run it using [the run sheet behind a successful pub event](/licensees-guide/how-to-run-successful-pub-events), and promote it as a local tradition from day one. Then hold the date for a year.

## How to measure it

Goodwill is hard to count, so count the things next to it. Track four numbers monthly: private hire and group enquiries, covers on the day of your ritual event, new names added to your mailing list at community events, and the number of guests who mention a community event when asked how they heard about you. Give each partnership its own booking link or a code word at the door if you can, and review the whole picture quarterly rather than weekly.

## Common mistakes

- **One-off charity nights with no follow-up.** Everyone feels good, nothing changes. Book the next one before people leave.
- **Partnering with groups that have no reach.** Enthusiasm is not an audience. Ask how many people are on their mailing list before you commit a night.
- **Running five ideas at once.** Two done properly beat five done thinly, and your team will tell you the same.
- **Giving away your best trading hours.** Free space on a Tuesday morning is generous. Free space on a Friday night is a mistake.
- **Making it about you.** If every post about a community event is really a post about your pub, people notice quickly.

## Quick checklist

- One visibility action live this week.
- Two local partners shortlisted and contacted.
- One monthly ritual chosen, dated and held for a year.
- Written agreement in place for any partnership involving money.
- Team briefed on the outreach story so they can answer questions at the bar.

## Mini FAQ

**Is outreach just charity?**
It is strategic relationship building. Measure bookings, group enquiries and repeat visits tied to each initiative, and be honest when one is not working.

**How do I staff outreach without extra hours?**
Use quieter dayparts, and partner with groups that bring their own helpers. Most community groups expect to set up their own room.

**How long before it shows in the takings?**
Expect three months before the pattern shifts and closer to six before it feels normal. The pubs that give up at week six are the ones that conclude it does not work.
