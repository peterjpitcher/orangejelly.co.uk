# Village Pub in a Dying Village? Survival Strategies That Actually Work

I run The Anchor in Stanwell Moor as a Greene King tenant. It is a small village hemmed in by Heathrow, the reservoir and the M25, and the village on its own was never going to pay our bills. That was true when we took it on and it is true now.

"Dying village" is usually the wrong diagnosis, though. The village may be shrinking. The catchment almost never is. Within a twenty minute drive of most rural pubs there are tens of thousands of people who would happily come to you, if they had a reason and if they could find you.

What follows is the order I would tackle it in. Do the arithmetic first, because it tells you how big the gap is and stops you spending money on the wrong fix.

## Step 1: Accept the new reality

Start with two numbers, not feelings.

The first is break-even. Add up everything that leaves the account whether or not a single person walks in: rent, wages, utilities, insurance, licences, accountancy, repairs. Say that comes to £6,500 a week. If your blended gross profit across wet and dry sits at 60%, you need £10,833 of sales every week just to stand still. That is a worked example, not your number, so run it with your own figures tonight.

The second is average spend per head. Your till knows: total sales divided by covers. Say it is £16, and remember that is a till figure with VAT in it. So put the VAT back on your £10,833 of net sales to get £13,000 through the till, divide by £16, and you need roughly 813 visits a week, about 116 a day, every day. Mixing a net sales target with a gross spend per head is the quiet way to flatter this sum by a fifth.

Now count the village honestly. Ward populations from the census are published free by the ONS and most councils put out a local area profile. Take the adult population, assume a generous one in five will ever use your pub, and assume they come twice a month. A village of 900 adults gives you 180 people making 360 visits a month, about 83 a week. Against 813.

That gap is the whole article. It is not a failure of hospitality, it is arithmetic, and no amount of being lovely closes it.

**This week:** run those two sums, then pull twelve months of till data and work out which days and dayparts actually cover their own labour. Our guide to [turning EPOS data into a revenue comeback](/licensees-guide/epos-data-revenue-comeback) walks through the reports worth running.

The mistake here is cutting hours to match the shrinking village. Closing a session that loses £40 feels sensible, but if you shut the sessions visitors could use, you have shrunk the only market capable of growing.

## Step 2: Become a destination, not a default

A destination pub has one reason to travel, not forty. Ask a stranger in the next village why they should drive to you. If three different people give three different answers, you do not have a reason, you have a menu.

Pick one hero and resource it properly:

- **A hero dish.** One thing done to a standard nobody within fifteen miles matches. Name it after the pub, photograph it well, put it top right of the menu where eyes land first.
- **A hero session.** Sunday lunch is the obvious one because it travels well and people book it in advance.
- **A hero setting.** A garden with a view, a fire, a genuinely dog friendly bar. Setting is the cheapest hero to build if you already have it.

Do the margin maths before you commit. Say a roast plate costs you £4.20 in food and you sell it at £18.95. That is £15.79 excluding VAT, so your gross profit is £11.59, about 73%. Comfortably above the 65% to 70% most pubs target on food, which gives you room to be generous with portions and still be viable. Again, an example: price yours off your own invoices.

The failure mode is adding rather than choosing. A struggling pub that bolts on tapas, curry night, burgers and a pie week has made its kitchen harder and its reason to visit weaker.

## Step 3: Build a multi-purpose model

Your building is empty for most of its waking hours. A village pub that only trades from six is running a very expensive storage unit until then.

Things that work, in rough order of how easy they are to start:

- **Daytime coffee and laptops.** You need proper wifi, power at half the tables, and a plain rule on the door such as laptops welcome until midday. Coffee GP is strong and it costs you almost nothing to open a room you are already heating.
- **Parcel collection.** Signing up as a pickup point for a carrier network pays only pennies per parcel, but it puts twenty people a day through your door who then see your menu board.
- **Post office outreach and a shop counter.** Where a village has lost its shop or post office, a few shelves and a couple of hours a week of counter service can make you genuinely essential.
- **Clubs and meeting space.** Parish council, walking group, book club, WI, knit and natter. Give them a room at no charge on a dead afternoon and take the drinks and cake.

Money exists for this. Pub is The Hub, the not-for-profit founded by King Charles III in 2001, offers grants of up to £6,000 plus specialist advice to licensees in rural, remote or deprived areas adding community services. That is real capital for a shop fit-out or a coffee machine, and it comes with people who have done it before.

Two cautions. Check your premises licence and your tenancy agreement before you add a retail use, and never let a daytime use make the evening pub feel like an office.

## Step 4: Invest in the visitor economy

Rural pubs sit on a market most of them ignore: people who are already out for the day.

- **Walkers and cyclists.** Get your pub named as the start or finish point on route apps and local walking group listings, then print a one page route card with your postcode, a what3words location and a stamp on the back for a free coffee on return. Talk to the parish footpath officer; they will tell you which paths actually get used.
- **Accommodation partners.** Every B&B, holiday let, campsite and small hotel within twenty minutes is sending guests somewhere for dinner. Walk in with menus and a simple deal: their guests get a free welcome drink, you get the referral. Ask them to put your card in the welcome folder.
- **Touring caravans.** Some pubs with a spare field host small five van sites through club exemption certificate schemes. That is a genuine trade builder because those guests eat with you. Speak to the clubs directly before assuming your field qualifies, as the rules on site licensing are specific.
- **Packages, not discounts.** A "walk and lunch" at a fixed price, or a "stay and dine" with a nearby B&B, sells better than money off and protects your margin. Our guide to [off-season revenue packages](/licensees-guide/survive-off-season-revenue-packages) has the pricing logic.

## Step 5: Strengthen digital presence

Rural trade is search trade. Nobody wanders past a village pub. They search "Sunday roast near me", "dog friendly country pub" or "pub with garden Staines", they look at photos, they check the opening hours, and they decide before they get in the car.

That makes your Google Business Profile the most valuable asset you own after the building. Get the categories right, keep hours accurate including bank holidays, add photos monthly, answer questions, and link straight to a booking page rather than a phone number. Our [Google Business Profile guide for pubs](/licensees-guide/google-business-profile-pub-guide) covers the settings that move the needle.

Then give your website a page for each thing people actually search: Sunday lunch, the garden, the function room, dog friendly, parking. One page per intent beats one clever page about everything.

This is where the effort compounds. At The Anchor we grew Google Search visibility by 828%, and that is the single change that turned a small village into a workable catchment. ## Step 6: Keep the community heart

Chasing visitors while quietly dropping the locals is the fastest way to end up with neither. Visitors come in waves. Locals are your Tuesday.

Protect one fixed weekly night that belongs to the village and never move it. Keep the darts team, the dominoes, the noticeboard, the collection tin. Learn names and use them. Run a simple loyalty card for people with a local postcode so regulars get recognised without you cutting your headline prices.

Then go and ask. A twenty minute conversation with the parish council about what the village has lost will hand you your next three ideas, and our guide to [reintroducing your pub to the community](/licensees-guide/community-outreach-reintroduce-pub) sets out how to run that properly.

## Common mistakes

- **Trying to serve everyone at once.** Pick one hero offer and one target visitor. Breadth reads as blandness.
- **Cutting opening hours before cutting costs.** Fix the cost base and the offer first. Closed doors cannot be marketed.
- **Discounting to fill the room.** A 20% discount on 60% gross profit costs you a third of your margin. Add value instead.
- **Ignoring online visibility.** If your hours are wrong on Google, nothing else in this article matters.
- **Dropping community traditions to look upmarket.** Keep the quiz, keep the meat raffle, raise the standard of everything around them.

## Quick checklist

- Break-even and spend per head calculated from real figures.
- One destination offer chosen, costed and photographed.
- One daytime use launched and staffed.
- Three accommodation or walking partnerships agreed.
- Google Business Profile fully completed with a booking link.
- One weekly locals' night ring-fenced in the diary.

## Mini FAQ

**How far should I target for visitors?**
Work to a twenty minute drive radius, and check on a map what is actually inside it before you spend on advertising.

**Can a village pub survive without food?**
It is difficult. Food is usually the reason people travel, and it is the only way most rural sites reach a workable spend per head.

**Should I put prices up when the village is struggling?**
Usually yes, on the right lines. Hold your entry level pint, and take the increase on food and premium drinks where the value is visible.
