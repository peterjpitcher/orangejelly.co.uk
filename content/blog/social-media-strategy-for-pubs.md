# The Complete Social Media Strategy Guide for Pubs in 2025

Social media works for a pub when it has one job: getting people through the door. Everything else is decoration.

Most pubs do not have a content problem. They have a system problem. They post when they remember, about whatever happened that day, with no way of telling whether any of it produced a single booking.

I run The Anchor in Stanwell Moor and I do our marketing myself, around service. This is the system I use, and it takes under an hour a week once it is set up.

## Step 1: Define the goal

Decide what social media should drive: bookings, event attendance, or awareness. One primary goal keeps the content focused.

Make it a number you can check. "More bookings" is a wish. "Ten more covers on a Wednesday by the end of the quarter" is a target that tells you what to post and, more usefully, what not to post.

Pick the goal that matches your weakest trading session, not your busiest. There is no point pushing Sunday lunch when Sunday is already full and Tuesday is dead.

## Step 2: Assign platform roles

Do not try to master all of them at once. Give each platform one job and ignore the rest until that one works.

| Platform | Its job | What to post | What to measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Facebook | Events and community | Event listings, what's on, local news, group posts | Event responses, messages, link clicks |
| Instagram | Atmosphere and food | Reels of the room, plates, the garden in good light | Shares, profile visits, link taps |
| Google Business Profile | Being found and chosen | Fresh photos, correct hours, offers, event posts | Calls, direction requests, website clicks |
| WhatsApp | Enquiries and group bookings | Replies, not broadcasts | Enquiries answered and converted |
| TikTok | Reach beyond your regulars | Short vertical clips, one idea each | Views, follows, bio link clicks |

One important correction to the usual advice: your Google Business Profile is social media, and for a pub it may be the highest-value channel you have. It is what people see when they search "pub near me" or your name. It takes event posts, photos and offers just like Facebook does, and the people looking at it are already deciding where to go tonight. Our [Google Business Profile guide for pubs](/guides/google-business-profile-pub-guide) covers how to set it up properly.

Also be realistic about Facebook. Organic reach on business pages is a fraction of your follower count now, so a post that only lives on your page will reach very few of the people who follow you. Facebook still earns its place for events and for local community groups, where a genuine, non-spammy post from a licensee still travels. There is more on that in our guide to [Facebook marketing for local pubs](/guides/facebook-marketing-local-pubs).

## Step 3: Build a simple calendar

A basic weekly rhythm is enough:

- Monday: what's on this week.
- Wednesday: a story or a staff highlight.
- Friday: social proof or an event reminder.

Fix the days and the times and do not negotiate with yourself about them. Consistency beats volume every time, because the algorithm and your regulars both learn when to expect you.

Then layer a monthly rhythm on top: one event push per month planned three weeks out, one seasonal menu or offer, and one community post (a charity night, a local team, a supplier you use). Put the whole thing in a wall planner in the office where your team can see it, not in an app only you open.

Plan around the calendar you already have. Bank holidays, school holidays, the local carnival, big sporting fixtures and the first genuinely warm weekend of the year are all predictable. For how the seasonal side fits together, see our [summer pub marketing playbook](/guides/summer-pub-marketing).

## Step 4: Create a content workflow

Batch content once a week. Take photographs during service and store them in a shared folder. This removes the daily stress.

Make it concrete. Give your team a shared album on the phone or a shared cloud folder, and ask for three photographs a shift: one plate, one room shot, one person doing something. Then spend twenty minutes on your quiet morning writing the week's three posts and scheduling them.

Shoot with the light coming through a window onto the subject, not behind it. Wipe the phone lens. Shoot vertical for stories and reels, and get closer than feels natural. Those four habits fix most pub photographs on their own, and there is more detail in our guide to [Instagram marketing for pubs](/guides/instagram-marketing-for-pubs).

If you want hands-on help building the system rather than doing it alone, our [pub marketing](/pub-marketing) is built specifically for pubs.

## Step 5: Track results that matter

Track bookings, messages, and link clicks. Likes are useful only if they lead to visits.

Set up three things this week. Put a booking link in every profile bio and use a tagged version of it (a UTM link) so your website analytics can tell you which channel sent the booking. Ask "how did you hear about us?" on the booking form. And keep a one-page sheet with four columns: week, what you posted, covers taken, enquiries received.

After eight weeks, that sheet will tell you more than any dashboard. You will see which post format actually fills tables, and you can do more of it. This is exactly how we grew table bookings by 403% at The Anchor: not by posting more, but by finding the handful of posts that worked and repeating them relentlessly.

Only once you know which posts convert should you spend money. Then boosting a proven post to a tight local radius for £10 to £20 is one of the cheapest marketing pounds in the trade.

## Give every post a job

Every post should lead somewhere. Before you publish, ask one question: what do you want someone to do? A nice photo of a pint in the sun is fine. A nice photo with a clear next step is better. Give each post one job:

- **Book a table**: link straight to your booking page.
- **View the menu**: send people to the summer or specials menu.
- **Message on WhatsApp**: for quick questions and group bookings.
- **Join an event**: point to the bookable moment, not just the poster.
- **Share with a friend**: ask, and people will.

Treat every post as part of the journey from interest to booking, not as content for its own sake. The most common failure I see is a beautiful photograph with no date, no price, no address and no link. Every post should answer what, when, where and what next, because a stranger who has never been in has none of that context.

## The rules that apply to pub posts

Your organic posts are marketing communications, so the CAP Code applies to them, not just to paid ads.

- **Alcohol and age.** Alcohol should not be advertised where more than 25% of the audience is under 18, anyone shown drinking or playing a significant role must be and appear to be over 25, and you must not suggest alcohol brings confidence, popularity or sexual success. Avoid anything that reads as encouraging fast or heavy drinking.
- **Photographs of people.** Ask before you post a recognisable customer, and take it down without argument if they change their mind. Get written consent for staff photographs while they work for you and agree what happens when they leave.
- **Offers.** If you post a price or a deal, state the terms and the end date on the post itself. Vague offers create arguments at the bar.
- **Music in videos.** Use the audio library inside the platform's business account tools, because tracks available to personal accounts are not always cleared for commercial use. This is separate from your PRS and PPL licences, which cover music played in the pub.

## Common mistakes

- Posting without a clear next step.
- An inconsistent schedule, then a burst of six posts in one day.
- Using only stock images instead of your actual room and your actual people.
- Talking about the pub rather than to the customer.
- Chasing follower counts from outside your catchment area.
- Never checking whether any of it produced a booking.

## Quick checklist

- One main goal chosen, tied to your weakest session.
- Platform roles defined, including Google Business Profile.
- Weekly calendar fixed to three set days.
- Shared photo folder set up and the team briefed.
- Booking link in every profile, with tracking on it.
- A one-page results sheet started this week.
