# Facebook Marketing for Local Pubs: Your Community Connection Goldmine

Facebook is still where local communities plan their week. The village group, the school-run parents, the darts team, the "what's on near me" thread: that is where your next fifty covers are already talking to each other.

Pubs that do badly on Facebook treat it as a noticeboard. They post a poster, get four likes, decide it does not work, and stop. Pubs that do well treat it as the place they are a neighbour first and a business second.

I run The Anchor in Stanwell Moor as a Greene King tenant. What follows is what I actually do, in the order I would do it if I were starting again on Monday morning.

## Step 1: Fix the basics on your page

Spend an hour making your page accurate before you post anything else. Most of the trade you lose on Facebook is lost here, quietly, by people who checked and left.

- **Opening hours, including separate kitchen hours.** Facebook shows "Open now" or "Closed" from what you entered. If food stops at 8pm but your page says you close at 11pm, you will get one-star reviews about a meal you never promised.
- **A booking link on the action button.** Set the button to "Book now" and send it straight to your booking form, not your homepage. Every extra click loses you covers.
- **Special hours for bank holidays and Christmas.** Enter them properly rather than mentioning them in a post that scrolls away by Tuesday.
- **Twenty current photos.** Phone camera, daylight, real food, the real bar, the garden, the fire lit. Delete anything from a previous chef or a previous carpet.
- **The map pin on your actual door**, and your name, address and phone number written identically to how they appear on your [Google Business Profile](/guides/google-business-profile-pub-guide). Mismatched details confuse both customers and search engines.

A quick test: hand your phone to someone who has never been in, open your page, and ask them to work out whether you serve food on a Wednesday evening and how they would book. If they hesitate, fix what made them hesitate.

## Step 2: Use events properly

A Facebook Event is not a poster. It is a reminder system that keeps working after you have gone to bed.

Create weekly events about ten to fourteen days ahead. Create the big ones, Christmas, a beer festival, a name act, four to six weeks ahead so people can move plans around them.

Then do the things most pubs skip:

- **Co-host the event.** Add your quiz host, band, charity or supplier as a co-host and it appears on their page and in front of their followers as well as yours. It costs nothing and takes a minute.
- **Use a landscape image with the date readable at thumbnail size.** Most people see the picture at the size of a postage stamp.
- **Add the booking link in the ticket field**, even for free events, so there is always a way to commit.
- **Post inside the event three times:** when you announce it, three days out, and on the morning itself with something live from the room.
- **Ask the team to press "Interested" and invite friends.** Attendees can invite their own friends, which is how an event reaches people who have never heard of you.

One warning. "Interested" is a maybe, not a booking. Take names for tables anyway. If you want the mechanics of building nights worth promoting in the first place, our guide on [running successful pub events](/guides/how-to-run-successful-pub-events) covers the format side.

## Step 3: Be present in local groups

Local groups are where the real footfall sits. Find five to eight that matter: the village or town noticeboard, the "spotted in" group, the parents' group, dog walkers, the local sports club, and the buy-and-sell page.

Read the rules before you post a word. Many groups allow business posts only on a named day, and some ban them outright. Message the admin, introduce yourself as the licensee, and ask what is allowed. Admins who know you are far more forgiving than admins who catch you.

Then give before you take. Answer the parking question. Tell someone which of your dishes is safe for a nut allergy. Confirm you are showing the England game. Share the road closure that affects everyone. Being the useful pub in the group is worth more than ten posters.

The mistake that gets people banned is dropping the identical graphic into eight groups on the same morning. Write each post fresh, in your own voice, and mention the pub as a fact rather than a pitch.

## Step 4: Run small, targeted ads

Boost the post that already worked organically rather than writing a new advert. If a Reel of the Sunday roast coming out of the pass got shared twelve times on its own, that is the one to put money behind.

Meta lets you target a radius as tight as one mile around your pub, but very small radii in thinly populated areas struggle to deliver, so 3 to 5 miles is the reliable starting point for most locals. Set the location, set a sensible age range, and let the creative do the rest. Meta's delivery now leans heavily on what your video and image signal about who should see them, so a strong local clip beats a clever stack of interests.

Here is the arithmetic, as a worked example rather than a promise. Say you spend £10 a week. If that brings in one extra table of four, spending £25 a head, that is £100 through the till. Take the VAT off and you are working with £83.33, so at roughly 65% gross margin you keep about £54 against £10 of ad spend. You do not need the ads to work miracles. You need them to work once a week.

The classic error is boosting to people who already like your page. They are coming anyway. Point the money at cold local reach.

## Step 5: Use Messenger for bookings

Make it effortless to ask a question, and answer fast.

- Turn on an instant reply that gives your hours and your booking link.
- Set up ready-made replies for the four questions you get every week: dogs, parking, allergens, and whether you take bookings.
- Answer within the hour during service. Facebook publishes a "typically replies within" badge, and a slow one costs you enquiries before anyone types.
- Assign one person per shift to own the inbox. Unread messages are unanswered customers.

Critically, a Messenger conversation is not a booking until it is written in the diary. Move it across, take a name and a phone number, and confirm.

Table bookings at The Anchor are up **403%**, and the single biggest lever was not clever copy. It was removing friction between someone deciding and someone booking.

## What to post: the content mix

Aim for three posts that give something for every one that asks for something.

| Post type | What it looks like | Why it earns reach |
|---|---|---|
| Short vertical video | Pulling a pint, plating a roast, lighting the fire | Video travels furthest on Facebook now |
| Real food photo | Shot near a window, no flash, no filter | Locals want to see your food, not a stock image |
| People | A staff birthday, a regular's 80th, the darts team | Faces get comments, comments get reach |
| What's on | One graphic a week with the whole week on it | Gets screenshotted and stuck on fridges |
| Proof | A screenshot of a five-star review, thanked properly | Social proof without boasting |

Whatever you post, reply to every comment within a few hours. Comments tell Facebook the post is worth showing to more people, and they cost you nothing but attention. The same instincts apply next door on Instagram, though the format differs; our [Instagram marketing guide for pubs](/guides/instagram-marketing-for-pubs) covers that.

## Weekly rhythm

- **Monday:** what is on this week, one graphic, booking link.
- **Wednesday:** a person or a plate. Something human, no ask.
- **Thursday:** the weekend push with a clear call to action.
- **Sunday:** something live from the room while it is busy.

Four posts. Fifteen minutes each. Batch the shooting on a quiet Tuesday and schedule the lot in Meta Business Suite.

## Measure what actually works

Reach is a vanity number. Bookings are the real one.

Once a month, open your page insights, sort your last 90 days of posts by reach, and look at the top three. Do more of exactly those. Then check the bottom three and stop making them.

For attribution, keep it crude but honest: use a distinct booking link for Facebook so you can count clicks, and train the team to ask "how did you hear about tonight?" and write the answer on the sheet. ## Common mistakes

- Posting without a call to action. Every post needs a next step, even if it is only "come down".
- Ignoring comments and messages, then wondering why reach fell.
- Stock photography. Everyone can tell, and it makes the real photos look fake too.
- Posting only when you are quiet. The algorithm rewards consistency, not panic.
- Running an event without ever posting inside it.

## Quick checklist

- Hours, kitchen hours, booking button and photos all current.
- Events created and co-hosted for every named night this month.
- Group rules read, admins introduced to, posts written fresh.
- £10 to £20 a week behind your best organic post, 3 to 5 mile radius.
- Messenger instant reply live and someone owning the inbox each shift.
- Monthly review of your top three and bottom three posts.
