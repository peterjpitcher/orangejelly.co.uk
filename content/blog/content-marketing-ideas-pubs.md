# Pub Content That Fills Seats: What to Post and When

Content marketing works for pubs because it turns your real story into demand. You do not need viral videos. You need consistent, human content that makes your pub feel like somewhere people want to be tonight.

The trap is treating content as advertising. Nobody follows a pub to read adverts. They follow because they want to feel part of the place, and then they book because you made it easy at the right moment.

This guide breaks content into four pillars, then gives you a weekly rhythm, a filming routine and a way to tell if it is actually working.

## Pillar 1: Story and identity

Story tells people who you are and why you exist. It is the pillar most pubs skip, and the reason so many feeds look interchangeable.

Write in the first person, as yourself, not as "the team at The Red Lion". Keep it short and specific. Nobody remembers "great food, great atmosphere". People remember that you changed the Sunday roast because a regular told you the beef was arriving cold.

Five prompts that always produce something usable:

- Why you took the pub on, and what it looked like the day you got the keys.
- The story behind one dish or one beer, including the supplier's name.
- A staff spotlight: three questions, one photo, their drink of choice.
- Something that went wrong and what you changed because of it.
- A piece of local history about the building or the street.

The mistake to avoid: writing a paragraph of corporate description with no person in it. If you could paste your post into another pub's feed and it would still make sense, rewrite it.

## Pillar 2: Proof and social life

Guests want to see other guests. An empty, beautifully lit room says "nobody comes here". A slightly noisy photo of a full bar at 8pm says "get down here".

Make it a routine rather than a decision. Take one photo from the doorway at your busiest moment every Friday and Saturday, and use the best one. That single habit produces 100 proof shots a year.

Other formats that work:

- Short clips of an event, cut to under 15 seconds, with the sound left on.
- A screenshot of a genuine review with your reply underneath.
- Customer photos you have asked permission to repost, credited by tagging them.
- Setup shots: the cellar, the pass, the chalkboard being written.

Reply to every review, good and bad. A calm reply to a poor review is read by far more people than the review itself, and it becomes proof of how you handle things.

## Pillar 3: Useful information

Useful content removes friction. Most people are not deciding whether to like you, they are deciding whether visiting will be easy.

List the questions you get asked at the bar and on the phone every week: parking, dogs, children, allergens, whether the match is on, what time the kitchen closes, whether you take bookings for six. Each one is a post, and each one should also be a permanent answer on your website and your Google Business Profile.

That last point matters more than most licensees expect. Social posts reach people who already know you exist. Search reaches people deciding right now. At The Anchor, our own pub, we grew Google Search visibility by 828%, and the foundation was answering the practical questions properly and keeping the profile current. Our [Google Business Profile guide for pubs](/licensees-guide/google-business-profile-pub-guide) walks through the setup.

Two habits worth building: update your hours the moment they change, including bank holidays, and write real alt text on every image so the post works for people using a screen reader.

## Pillar 4: Offers and events

This is where content turns into trade. A good post has one clear action and one clear reason to act now.

Weak: "Quiz night Thursday, everyone welcome!"

Better: "Quiz starts 8pm Thursday. Six tables left, teams of up to six, £2 a head and the winners take the pot. Book: [link]."

The second version has a time, a limit, a price, a prize and a link. Scarcity only works when it is true, so count the tables before you post the number.

Rotate the reasons so the feed does not become one long sales pitch: an event, a new dish, a seasonal drink, a group booking window opening. Keep offers to roughly one post in four, and always end with the same booking link so people learn where to tap.

## Where to post, and what each channel is for

Posting the same thing everywhere is fine. Expecting the same result from each is not.

| Channel | What it is good at | What to put there |
|---|---|---|
| Facebook | Reaching local adults, events, community groups | What is on this week, events, longer stories, community news |
| Instagram | Atmosphere and food, reaching people who do not follow you yet | Short vertical video, food close-ups, room shots, stories |
| Google Business Profile | People searching for somewhere nearby, right now | Weekly update post, fresh photos, accurate hours and links |
| Email or SMS | The people who already like you, with the highest conversion | Event invitations, booking windows, seasonal menus |
| Your website | The place every link lands and every booking completes | Menus, opening hours, booking, the answers to common questions |

Two channel-specific notes. Local Facebook groups are the cheapest reach a pub has, but read the group rules and speak to the admin before posting anything promotional; get that wrong and you lose the channel. The rules that make this work are in our guide to [Facebook marketing for local pubs](/licensees-guide/facebook-marketing-local-pubs). On Instagram, short vertical video gets shown to people who do not follow you, while a photo mostly reaches people who already do, so use video when you want new faces and photos when you want to talk to regulars. Our [Instagram marketing guide for pubs](/licensees-guide/instagram-marketing-for-pubs) covers the formats.

An email or SMS list is the only audience you own outright. Collect it properly, with clear consent and an easy way to opt out, and treat it as your highest-value channel. Our guide to [email marketing for pub retention](/licensees-guide/email-marketing-pub-retention) covers the consent rules and the sequence.

## Simple weekly rhythm

- **Monday:** what is on this week, with times and the booking link.
- **Wednesday:** a story or staff feature.
- **Friday:** social proof, the doorway photo or an event recap.
- **Sunday:** a teaser for next week, ideally as a short video.

Four posts, one per pillar, every week. If you can only manage three, drop the Sunday teaser and keep the other three fixed. A predictable rhythm beats a burst of ten posts followed by three quiet weeks.

## Shoot a week of content in twenty minutes

Content dies from being a daily decision. Make it a weekly job with a shot list.

Pick a quiet slot, ideally late morning with the doors open and daylight coming in. Work through eight shots: the bar from the doorway, one dish plated, the chef or a bartender at work, a close-up of a pint being pulled, the chalkboard, the garden or the fire depending on the season, a ten-second walk through the room, and one clip of you talking to camera about what is on this week.

Three rules that lift the quality more than any equipment: film vertically, keep the background tidy (clear the empty glasses and the blue roll), and stand so the light is on your subject rather than behind it. Use the app's own music library rather than adding a track from your phone.

That gives you a folder to draw from all week, and a spare when a night goes quiet and you need something up fast.

## How to tell whether it is working

Judge content on trade, not applause. Check the same four things every Monday morning:

- Bookings made through your link in the last seven days.
- Calls and direction requests on your Google Business Profile.
- Shares, and how many people bookmark a post, which signal intent far better than likes do.
- What guests say at the table when you ask how they heard about tonight.

Ask every team member to note the answer to that last question for a fortnight. It is the cheapest attribution you will ever get, and it usually surprises people.

Review on a four-week rolling basis. Single posts are noisy. Patterns are not.

## Common mistakes

- **No call to action.** If the post does not say what to do next, it is decoration.
- **Only menus and opening hours.** Necessary, but it gives nobody a reason to follow you.
- **Waiting for perfect photos.** A slightly imperfect post today beats a polished one that never gets made.
- **Posting after the moment has passed.** Thursday's quiz needs promoting on Monday and again on Thursday morning, not at 7.45pm.
- **Ignoring comments.** Every unanswered question in the comments is a booking someone else took.

## Quick checklist

- All four pillars covered across the week.
- One clear call to action and one booking link per post.
- At least one proof post every week.
- Google Business Profile updated weekly and hours correct.
- A twenty-minute filming slot in the rota, same day every week.

## Mini FAQ

**Should I boost posts with paid spend?**
Only once the organic rhythm is steady. Boosting a post with no clear action just buys you views. Put a small budget behind the posts that already earn bookings, and leave the rest alone.
