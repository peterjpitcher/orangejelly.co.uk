# Email Marketing for Pubs: Build a Database That Drives Repeat Business

A great visit means nothing if you never see the guest again. Email gives you a direct way to bring people back without paying for reach every single time.

It is also the only channel you actually own. Facebook can halve your reach overnight and never tell you why. Your email list cannot be taken away, throttled, or repriced by someone else's algorithm.

I run The Anchor in Stanwell Moor as a Greene King tenant, and the list is the one marketing asset here that nobody else controls. What follows is how I would build one from nothing.

Email works even better alongside a [loyalty scheme that fills your pub](/licensees-guide/build-loyalty-scheme-fill-pub). The two reinforce each other: the scheme gives people a reason to hand over an address, and the emails give you a reason to use it.

## Step 1: Build the list the right way

Not all addresses are equal. Ranked by how likely they are to produce an actual booking:

1. **The booking form.** Add an opt-in tick box under the phone number field. These are your best contacts because they have already chosen you once.
2. **Event sign-ups.** Anyone who registers for a quiz, tasting or Christmas party has told you exactly what they like.
3. **Loyalty registration.** A card or app sign-up carries a natural reason to collect an address.
4. **A QR card on the table.** One field, email only. Every extra field you add costs you sign-ups, so ask for the first name later.
5. **WiFi login.** Volume, but the weakest intent, and the most legally fiddly. More on that below.

Give the team a one-line script rather than leaving them to improvise: "We send one short email a week with what's on, want me to put you on it?" That is it. No clipboard, no speech.

The arithmetic is more encouraging than most licensees expect. As an illustration, say you do 40 covers on an average night, you ask everyone, and one in five says yes. That is eight addresses a night. Over five trading nights that is 40 a week, and inside a year you have a list of well over a thousand local people who have already eaten your food.

Never buy a list. Bought addresses do not know you, will not open, and will wreck the sending reputation that gets your real emails delivered.

## The law: what you can and cannot send

This is the part most pub marketing advice skips, and it is not complicated once you know the shape of it. UK email marketing sits under two rules: UK GDPR for holding the data, and PECR for sending the messages.

You need either specific consent or the soft opt-in.

- **Consent** must be freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous. That means a tick box the person ticks themselves. Pre-ticked boxes are not consent.
- **The soft opt-in** lets you email an existing customer who bought from you, or negotiated to buy, where you are marketing something similar, and where you gave them a simple way to opt out both at the point you collected the address and in every message since.

Two practical points that catch pubs out:

**WiFi.** You can ask for an email address to grant access, but you cannot make marketing consent a condition of getting online. Use two separate tick boxes: one to connect, one to hear from you.

**Records.** You need to be able to show when and how someone opted in. Any decent platform stores this automatically, which is one good reason not to run your list out of a spreadsheet and your own mail client.

Also link a privacy notice wherever you collect addresses, put a working unsubscribe in every send, and set a retention rule. Something like removing contacts who have not engaged in 24 months is both tidy and good for deliverability. This is a plain-English summary rather than legal advice: the ICO's guidance on direct marketing using electronic mail is the source to check if you are unsure.

## Step 2: Create a welcome sequence

Write three emails once, set them to send automatically, and leave them alone.

- **Immediately: the welcome.** Thank them, say exactly what they will get and how often, and give them one genuinely useful thing. How to book, where to park, whether dogs are welcome.
- **Day three: your signature.** The hero dish, the house beer, the thing you want to be known for. One photograph, one paragraph, one button.
- **Day ten: a specific invitation.** Not "come and see us". A named event with a date and a booking link.

The mistake here is a welcome email that only says "thanks for subscribing". That is the most-opened email you will ever send. Do not waste it on a receipt.

## Step 3: Choose a weekly rhythm

One email, same day, same time. Tuesday late morning and Thursday afternoon both work well for us. Try both, keep whichever gets more clicks through to your booking page, and then leave it alone.

Keep the shape identical every week so it becomes furniture:

- A subject line under about 45 characters, specific rather than clever.
- One main thing at the top with a photo.
- Three lines of what else is on.
- One button that goes to your booking form.

Write to one person, not to a list. "The quiz is back on Thursday" beats "We are delighted to announce the return of our popular quiz night". Nobody finishes a newsletter. Everybody finishes six sentences.

## Automate the messages that pay for themselves

The scheduled weekly email builds the habit. The triggered ones do the heavy lifting, because they arrive exactly when they are relevant.

| Trigger | When it sends | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Booking confirmation | Immediately | Confirms the detail and cuts confusion |
| Booking reminder | 24 hours before | Gives an easy way to cancel or amend |
| Birthday | Seven days before | Prompts a group booking, not a solo pint |
| Lapsed guest | 90 days with no visit | Brings back people drifting away |
| Post-event | The morning after | Thanks them and dates the next one |

The reminder is the one to build first. Booking no-shows at The Anchor are down **89%**, and the mechanism is unglamorous: confirm the booking, remind the guest the day before, and make it effortless for them to tell you if plans have changed. An empty table you knew about at 4pm is a table you can resell.

For the lapsed-guest trigger, the tone matters more than the offer. A short, warm "we have not seen you in a while, here is what has changed" outperforms a discount almost every time. ## Step 4: Segment lightly

You do not need a sophisticated data model. Two or three groups will do:

- **Regulars.** People who visit monthly or more. Give them first refusal on tickets rather than discounts.
- **Event attendees.** People who came for a quiz, tasting or live music. Tell them about the next one of the same kind.
- **Families and lunch guests.** Weekend daytime people, who want completely different messages from your Friday night crowd.

Add one more list that is not really a segment: a suppression group. Anyone who has not opened or clicked in six months gets one honest re-permission email asking whether they still want to hear from you, and if they do not respond, they come off. A smaller list that lands in inboxes is worth far more than a big one that lands in spam.

## Step 5: Measure the basics

Be careful with open rates. Apple's Mail Privacy Protection loads images automatically for a large share of recipients, which inflates opens and makes them a soft signal at best. Use them to spot a trend, never to judge a single send.

Track these instead:

- **Click rate to your booking page.** The honest measure of whether the email did its job.
- **Bookings that came from the email.** Use a distinct booking link in your emails so you can count them, or a phrase to mention at the bar.
- **Unsubscribe rate.** A spike after one send tells you something specific went wrong, usually frequency or an offer that felt like a hard sell.

Do not chase published industry benchmarks. Judge each send against your own last four. If clicks and bookings are rising, the email is working.

## 90-day plan

- **Month 1:** get the opt-in live on the booking form and on table cards, brief the team on the one-line ask, and write the three welcome emails.
- **Month 2:** establish the weekly send. Same day, same time, four weeks without fail. Build the booking reminder automation.
- **Month 3:** split into two or three segments, add the birthday and lapsed-guest triggers, and test two different offers on the same session.

Ninety days in, you should have a list that grows every week without anyone thinking about it, and one automated email that quietly protects your covers.

## Common mistakes

- Sending only when you are quiet, so every email reads as a plea.
- No clear call to action, or five competing ones.
- Long newsletters nobody finishes.
- Discounting by reflex. Priority booking and added value protect your margin far better.
- Letting the list go stale, then wondering why delivery has collapsed.
- Collecting addresses with no privacy notice and no record of consent.

## Quick checklist

- Opt-in live on the booking form, on table cards and on the WiFi splash page, with separate tick boxes.
- Privacy notice linked wherever you collect an address.
- Three-email welcome sequence written and automated.
- Weekly email scheduled on a fixed day and time.
- Booking reminder automation live 24 hours before every booking.
- Clicks and actual bookings tracked, not just opens.
