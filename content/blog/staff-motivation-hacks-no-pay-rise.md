# Staff Motivation Hacks When You Can't Afford Pay Rises

A wage freeze does not have to mean a motivation crash. People stay for respect, growth and clarity as much as cash, and keeping them costs far less than [hiring and training replacements](/licensees-guide/pub-recruitment-hiring-bar-staff).

The National Living Wage rose to £12.71 an hour for workers aged 21 and over in April 2026. For a lot of us that increase swallowed the entire wage budget before we got anywhere near rewarding the people who actually earned it. So the question stops being "how do I pay more" and becomes "what else do people want, and can I give it to them without cash".

At The Anchor the answer has mostly been time, information and respect. Here is the order I would tackle it in, because two of these are worth more than all the clever ideas underneath them.

## Fix the rota before you try anything clever

Nothing burns goodwill faster than a rota that lands late and then changes. People plan childcare, second jobs and their lives around it. A publican who moves a Saturday shift on Thursday night has just cost someone money, and no amount of praise repairs that.

Set a fixed slot: rota out every Wednesday by 10am, covering the fortnight starting the following Monday, posted in the same place every time. Put it in your own calendar as a recurring task and treat it like a delivery you cannot miss.

This is also where the law is heading. The Employment Rights Act 2025 introduces a duty to give reasonable notice of shifts and to pay workers when a shift is cancelled, moved or cut at short notice, with the specifics due in regulations expected from 2027. Consultation options for the notice period run from one to four weeks. If you are already at a settled two weeks, the change costs you nothing.

**Mistake to avoid:** amending the rota in the team WhatsApp group without asking. Change it in the document, then ask the affected person directly.

## Get the tips right, in writing

Since 1 October 2024 the Employment (Allocation of Tips) Act 2023 has required employers to pass on 100% of qualifying tips to workers, with no deductions other than those tax law requires. Allocation has to be fair and transparent, tips must reach people by the end of the month after they were received, and where tips are paid more than occasionally you need a written policy available to everyone at that site, plus records of what was allocated.

Most pubs I visit do the right thing and have never written it down. That gap is where suspicion grows. One side of A4 on the staff noticeboard covering who shares, on what basis (hours worked, role weighting, whether the kitchen is included), and when it lands, removes the single biggest source of behind-the-bar resentment. It is free, and it is already your legal duty.

## 1. Make recognition daily

Do not hold praise back for meetings. Recognise great service in the moment, specifically, and where other people can hear it.

Example: "You handled that complaint perfectly, you kept your voice level and you offered the solution before they asked. Thank you."

Build it into the pre-shift line-up. Sixty seconds, every shift, name one thing someone did on the last one. Then write it in a note on your phone. When that person asks for a reference, a promotion or a pay rise you cannot yet give, you will have twenty specific examples instead of a vague sense that they are good.

## 2. Offer choice and flexibility

Since 6 April 2024 every employee has had the right to request flexible working from day one, with two requests permitted in any 12-month period, a two-month deadline for your decision, and a duty to consult before you refuse. Flexible working covers hours, days and shift patterns, not just working from home, so it lands squarely on hospitality rotas.

Do not wait to be asked. Once a quarter, ask everyone the same two questions: which shifts suit your life, and which ones are a struggle. Then build the rota around the answers where you can.

Publish a swap rule so people are not asking your permission at 11pm: swaps are fine if both parties agree, both are trained for the role, and it is posted in the group at least 48 hours ahead. Give first pick of Christmas and New Year dates by a published rule, such as rota reliability over the previous quarter, so it never looks like favouritism.

**Mistake to avoid:** informal flexibility for the people you happen to like. That reads as a two-tier pub and it will cost you your quieter, steadier staff first.

## 3. Invest in development time

Twenty minutes before service, once a week, is enough. Rotate the topics: line cleaning and why it changes the pint, three wine styles and what to say about them, coffee extraction, allergen questions, and your [upsell scripts](/licensees-guide/upselling-secrets-training-scripts).

You do not have to deliver these yourself. Your brewery business development manager, your wine supplier and your coffee roaster will almost all run a free session for your team, because it sells more of their product. Ask three of them this week and you have filled a quarter of the calendar for nothing.

Once a year, fund one person through their personal licence qualification. A few hundred pounds buys the pub cover and buys that person a qualification they keep forever. That is the sort of thing people remember about who they worked for.

**Mistake to avoid:** training only when something has gone wrong. It turns learning into a punishment.

## 4. Use micro-incentives, and get the tax right

Small rewards beat promises. A free meal after a hard week, a taxi home after a late close, a staff night out sponsored by a supplier rep, a bottle from the back bar for the person who covered two sicknesses.

Two rules worth knowing before you start. Free or subsidised meals eaten on the premises are generally exempt from tax and National Insurance if they are on a reasonable scale and open to all staff at that site, and HMRC treats free tea and coffee the same way. Separately, HMRC's trivial benefits exemption covers non-cash gifts costing up to £50 including VAT, but only where the gift is not cash or a cash voucher, is not contractual, and is not a reward for services.

That last condition catches more people than any other. A prize handed over explicitly for hitting a sales target is a reward for services and is taxable. Framing matters, so check your own arrangement with your accountant before you build it into a routine.

Worked example, purely illustrative: a proper shift meal that costs you £2.20 in food, given to twelve staff across four shifts a week, is about £106 a week in food cost. Split twelve ways across wage packets that is under £9 a head and nobody would notice. Served hot, on a plate, in a break that actually happens, it is one of the most talked-about things you do.

## 5. Share the numbers

When the team understands the targets, and how [wages fit into the weekly numbers](/licensees-guide/pub-wages-labour-costs-guide), they stop seeing an upsell as a trick and start seeing it as the thing that pays for their hours.

Put a whiteboard in the cellar corridor and update it every Monday with four figures: last week's wet sales, last week's food sales, covers, and this week's target. Add one line on what you are trying differently.

Share the trading numbers. Do not share your rent, your drawings or anybody's individual wage. The point is to make the target visible, not to make people anxious about the lease.

## 6. Upgrade the staff experience

Simple, unglamorous changes matter more than gestures:

- A staff area with somewhere to sit that is not a beer crate, a working lock on the door, and a kettle.
- A uniform that fits and gets replaced when it wears out, not when it falls apart.
- Water available on every shift, and breaks that actually get taken.
- The rota out on time, every time.

**Mistake to avoid:** spending £400 on new signage for the car park while the staff toilet still has no lock. Your team notices the order you spend money in.

## Give people a ladder they can see

Write down three named steps: bar team, shift leader, duty manager. Under each, list four or five things a person must be able to do to hold that title. Line cleaning unsupervised. Cashing up. Running a booking sheet. Handling a refusal of service.

Even with the pay frozen, a real title with real responsibility and the training to go with it is worth having, and it tells people they are somewhere with a future.

Then hand over genuine ownership. One person runs the socials. One person owns the cellar. One person owns [events from planning to follow-up](/licensees-guide/how-to-run-successful-pub-events), with a budget and the authority to spend it. Autonomy is the thing the chain down the road cannot copy, and when the money does come back you already know exactly who to pay first.

## Have the stay conversation

Do not wait for the resignation. Once a quarter, fifteen minutes each, three questions: what makes a good shift here, what makes a bad one, and what would you change if you ran the place.

Write the answers down. Act on one of them within a fortnight, and tell the person you did it because they said so. That single loop does more for morale than any incentive scheme.

The same applies in the kitchen, where the warning signs usually appear weeks before anyone hands in notice. If you have ever had a [chef walk out mid-service](/licensees-guide/kitchen-nightmares-chef-quits), you will recognise the pattern in hindsight.

## Common mistakes

- Using empty slogans instead of real recognition.
- Changing rotas last minute.
- Training only when something goes wrong.
- Promising a pay rise "when trade picks up" with no date and no trigger. Say what would have to be true instead, in numbers.
- Rewarding the loudest person rather than the most reliable one.

## Your first 30 days

| Week | Do this |
|------|---------|
| 1 | Set the fixed rota slot, publish two weeks ahead. Write the one-page tips policy and put it on the noticeboard. |
| 2 | Start the 60-second recognition slot at every line-up. Book two supplier-led training sessions. |
| 3 | Ask every team member the two shift-preference questions. Put the whiteboard up with last week's numbers. |
| 4 | Write the three-step ladder. Hand one area of the business to one person, with a budget. |

## Mini FAQ

**What if someone asks for more money anyway?**
Answer honestly with the ladder. Tell them what the next step is, what they need to be able to do to reach it, and what would have to be true in the trading numbers before you can fund it.
