# How to Win Back Locals After Months of Slow Trade

When regulars drift away it feels personal, and it usually is not. People rarely make a decision to stop coming. They miss a week, then a fortnight, then they get out of the habit and the pub quietly falls off their mental list of options.

That is good news, because habits are easier to restart than opinions are to change. What it needs is a clear story, direct invitations to named occasions, and thirty days of visible activity so that the neighbourhood has a reason to look again.

I run The Anchor in Stanwell Moor as a Greene King tenant, and this is the sequence I would run, in order, with the parts most licensees skip flagged as I go.

## Step 0: Fix the reason they left before you invite anyone back

Do this first. A warm invitation to a cold, scruffy room simply confirms the decision people already made.

Walk in as a guest on a Thursday evening. Time how long it takes for someone to acknowledge you. Order a pint and taste it properly. Sit at three different tables and check whether they are sticky and whether the seat is cold. Use the toilets. Read your own menu as if you had never seen it.

Nine times out of ten the honest list is short and unglamorous: line cleaning has slipped, the heating comes on too late, the music is wrong, one member of staff is quietly rude, the menu has fifteen dishes and the kitchen can only do six well. Beer quality is worth a hard look on its own, because it is the single fastest way to lose a wet-led local without ever hearing a complaint. Our [cellar management and beer quality guide](/guides/cellar-management-beer-quality-guide) is the checklist I would run against.

Give yourself two weeks to fix the basics. If money is tight, [rebooting the atmosphere on a small budget](/guides/reboot-pub-atmosphere-on-budget) gets you most of the way with lighting, layout and warmth rather than a refit.

## Step 1: Own the story

People support progress when you invite them into it. They do not support a long apology, and they switch off entirely when a licensee blames the economy, the weather or the brewery.

Write three sentences: what has changed, what has stayed the same, and what happens next.

> "We have tightened the menu to eight dishes we can genuinely do well, the cask lines are back on a weekly clean, and the fire is lit from four o'clock. Same team, same prices. From Thursday the third we are running a locals night every week, and we would love you to come and mark our homework."

Say it in four places: a post on Facebook, a post on your Google Business Profile, a printed card by the till, and out loud from behind the bar. The last one matters most. Every member of staff should be able to say it in their own words without reading it.

## Step 2: Build a lapsed regulars list

You almost certainly have more contacts than you think, scattered across six places. Spend an afternoon pulling them into one spreadsheet.

- Booking system exports, including cancelled and no-show bookings.
- Function and Christmas enquiries from the past two years, whether they booked or not.
- Any old email list, however neglected.
- EPOS loyalty or tab records.
- Facebook and Instagram followers who used to comment and have gone quiet.
- Staff memory. Sit the team down and write down the names of every group that used to come in. The darts lot, the Sunday walkers, the Tuesday dominoes crowd. Names beat numbers.

Record where each contact came from, because that determines what you are allowed to send them. Under the PECR rules, the soft opt-in generally lets you email or text people whose details you collected while selling them something, provided you offered them a way to refuse marketing at the time and you offer it in every message since. Marketing texts need a simple STOP instruction. If a mobile number came off a booking form with no mention of marketing, do not add it to a bulk text: phone that person or catch them in the pub instead.

Never buy a list. It will not contain your locals and it will damage your standing with both the ICO and your neighbours. Treat this spreadsheet as the foundation for proper [email marketing built on retention](/guides/email-marketing-pub-retention) rather than a one-off blast.

## Step 3: Send personal invitations

The rule is simple: the more personal the channel, the higher the response, and the more effort it takes. Work down the ladder, spending your best effort on the people who mattered most.

| Channel | Who it is for | Effort | What to say |
|---|---|---|---|
| Face to face | People you still see around the village | Low | "We are doing a locals night on the third, come and have a look" |
| Phone call | Former weekly regulars and group organisers | High | Ask how they are first, invite second |
| Handwritten card | Big spenders and function bookers | High | Six lines, signed, with a date on it |
| Text | Opted-in contacts | Low | One sentence, one date, one link |
| Email | The wider list | Low | Short, personal tone, single call to action |

Name the occasion and name the date. "Pop in sometime" gives nobody a reason to act. Compare these two:

> "Hi Sam, we have missed you. We are running a locals night next Thursday the third, and I have put your name against the corner table if you fancy it. Peter."

That works because it is specific, it is from a person, and it hands over something that already has their name on it.

Do not mass-BCC an email that opens with "Dear customer". Do not send the same message three times in a week. Two or three touch points across a month is plenty.

## Step 4: Create a 30-day drumbeat

One event does not rebuild a habit. Four weeks of visible activity does, because it gives people repeated chances to say yes and gives your regulars something to tell other people about.

- **Week one:** the locals night. Keep it simple and warm. Free bar snacks, the fire on, no ticketing.
- **Week two:** a [community collaboration that reintroduces the pub](/guides/community-outreach-reintroduce-pub). Host the running club, the school PTA, the parish council, anyone with their own audience.
- **Week three:** a new menu highlight or a tasting. Something with news value that the local Facebook groups will repeat for you.
- **Week four:** a small event with a date that repeats. This is the one you want to become permanent.

Photograph all four. Four weeks of pictures of a busy room does more for your reputation than any amount of writing about how much things have improved.

## Step 5: Celebrate their return

This is where most win-back campaigns quietly fail. People come back once, nobody notices, and they do not come back again.

Brief the team every shift with the names of who is expected. Reserve tables properly with a card on the table. Remember the order: "still the Guinness?" is worth more than any voucher. If someone has been away a long time, a drink on the house at the right moment lands well, but only if it comes with a conversation, not instead of one.

Confirm every booking the day before. Booking no-shows fell by 89% at The Anchor once we tightened up confirmations, and a table that stays empty on a relaunch night costs you twice: the covers, and the atmosphere.

## Step 6: Keep the loop alive

Momentum decays. The month after the drumbeat is when it either becomes normal or fades back.

Fix one recurring night in the diary and never move it. Keep collecting contacts at the point of booking, with a clear opt-in line. Then give the habit somewhere to land with a [simple loyalty scheme](/guides/build-loyalty-scheme-fill-pub) so returning is rewarded quietly rather than begged for.

Book the next invitation before the current one has happened. That is the whole trick.

## What to measure

- **Contacts reached**, split by channel, so you learn which one your locals actually respond to.
- **Returned names**, counted off the booking sheet against your lapsed list. This is the real score.
- **Second visits within 30 days.** One visit is curiosity, two is a habit restarting.
- **Weekly covers and net sales**, compared with the same four weeks before you started.
- **Opt-outs.** A handful is normal. A spike means you are sending too often or to the wrong people.

## Common mistakes

- **Waiting for locals to return on their own.** They will not. Somebody has to make the first move and it has to be you.
- **Inviting people back before the basics are fixed.** You get one look from a lapsed regular. Do not waste it.
- **Announcing big changes with no follow through.** Promise one thing you can definitely deliver rather than five you cannot.
- **Running one relaunch night and stopping.** The second and third weeks are where the habit forms.
- **Discounting instead of recognising.** A blanket offer attracts deal-hunters. A reserved table with their name on it attracts the person you actually want.
- **Letting the list go cold again.** If you only contact people when trade is bad, they learn what your messages mean.

## Quick checklist

- Guest walk-through done and the basics fixed.
- Three-sentence story written, and every member of staff can say it.
- Lapsed list built in one place, with the source recorded against each contact.
- Consent and opt-out position checked before any bulk message.
- Personal invitations sent, best channel to the best customers.
- Four-week calendar in the diary with dates, not intentions.
- Team briefed nightly on who is expected back.
- One recurring night fixed permanently.

## Mini FAQ

**How many times should I invite them?**
Two or three direct touch points across a month. More than that reads as desperation and pushes people to opt out.

**What if a former regular left because of a row?**
Deal with it directly and privately, once. A short call that acknowledges what happened without relitigating it will bring back more people than any campaign.
