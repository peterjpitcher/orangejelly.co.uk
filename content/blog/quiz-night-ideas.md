# Quiz Night Ideas: 26 Formats That Pack Your Pub Every Week

A great quiz night is not about more questions. It is about variety, pacing, and giving people a reason to come back next week rather than a reason to have come once.

The pubs that get this right treat the quiz as a weekly habit they are building, not an event they are staging. The format stays familiar enough that regulars know where they are, and changes just enough that nobody gets bored.

Below are 26 formats in four clusters, plus the legal bits, the money and the hosting details that decide whether teams come back.

## Cluster 1: Format upgrades

These are structural changes. They cost nothing and they fix the two most common complaints about pub quizzes: it dragged, and it felt the same as last week.

- **Shorter rounds with clear breaks.** Eight questions a round, not fifteen. Announce the break before it starts so people go to the bar rather than sitting still.
- **A rotating picture round.** Change the theme monthly, not weekly, so it stays a familiar fixture. Put the sheets on the tables when people sit down so early arrivals have something to do.
- **A weekly themed round.** One round in eight that changes: this week the 1980s, next week food and drink, the week after local history.
- **The joker.** Teams nominate one round before it starts to count double. It rewards self-knowledge and it keeps a team who bombed round two in the game.
- **The wipeout round.** All correct answers score double, one wrong answer scores zero. High drama, and it stops a runaway leader.
- **Connections.** Five questions whose answers share a hidden link, with a bonus point for spotting it.
- **The handicap.** New teams get two bonus points for their first three weeks, so newcomers do not give up.

## Cluster 2: Interactive rounds

Interaction keeps energy high and, more usefully, it moves people around the room and past the bar.

- **Music intros.** Ten clips of five seconds each. Needs a music licence, covered below.
- **Reverse audio.** The same clip played backwards. Harder than it sounds and very funny.
- **Closest to.** Estimation questions with no exact answer needed: how tall, how heavy, how many. Nobody is ever completely out of it.
- **Lightning round.** Sixty seconds, questions read fast, answers shouted or written. Use it to lift the room after the break.
- **The taste test.** Four crisps, four cheeses or four spirits in the same mixer. Ties straight into bar spend.
- **The physical round.** One member per team, a beer mat flip or a darts throw. Thirty seconds of noise that resets the whole room.

Keep interactive rounds to two per quiz. Three and the evening stops feeling like a quiz.

## Cluster 3: Theme nights

Run a full theme once a month. Any more often and the novelty stops being novel, and your regulars start feeling like the quiz is not for them.

- **The 90s night.** A safe bet if your regulars are in their thirties and forties.
- **Local history night.** The one that gets people talking across tables rather than only to their own team. Ask your oldest regulars for questions.
- **Sports special.** Time it to a tournament, not to a random Tuesday.
- **Film and TV.** Easy to source, easy to make visual.
- **Christmas special.** Bigger prize, teams book in advance, and it fills a week in December that would otherwise sit quiet between the party bookings.
- **Charity special.** Raises money and brings in people who have never been in before.
- **The early quiz.** A shorter, family-friendly version at 6pm during school holidays.

## Cluster 4: Prize and loyalty mechanics

This is the cluster most pubs get wrong, and the one that decides whether you have a quiz or a habit.

- **Weekly bar tab, not cash.** A £40 bar tab costs you the stock, not the £40. Take the VAT off first and you are giving away about £33 of net sales, so at a wet gross profit of 55% to 65% the stock costs you somewhere between £12 and £15. Cash walks out of the door.
- **A monthly leaderboard on a chalkboard.** Visible, updated in front of people, argued about. That is the point.
- **A season with a final.** Run twelve weeks, then a champions night. It gives people a reason to come in week nine when they would otherwise skip.
- **The wooden spoon.** A tiny prize for last place, usually a round of a specific drink. It keeps weaker teams coming back, and weaker teams are usually the ones who drink the most.
- **Team of the month photo** behind the bar. Free, and people bring friends to see it.
- **A rollover bonus question.** One hard question each week for a prize that grows if nobody gets it. Keep it a question of knowledge, not a draw, for the reasons below.

## Get the legal bits right before you print

None of this is complicated, but two things catch pubs out.

**A straight quiz is a test of skill, so it sits outside the gaming rules in the Gambling Act 2005.** The moment the winner is decided purely by chance, you are running something different. It is safest to avoid settling a tie with a coin toss, a card cut or a name out of a hat when there is a prize attached. Use a "closest to" tiebreak question instead, which keeps the whole night a contest of skill. If you want to run a raffle alongside the quiz, that is a lottery, and lotteries cannot be run for private profit, so it needs to be for a good cause with tickets sold and drawn at the event. If you are unsure how your particular set-up reads, your licensing authority will tell you.

**Recorded music needs a licence.** If you run a music round, you are playing recorded music in a public setting, which normally requires TheMusicLicence from PPL PRS. That is a single joint licence covering PRS for Music, which represents songwriters and publishers, and PPL, which represents performers and record labels. Most pubs already hold it for background music, but check it reflects what you actually do. The cost varies by venue type, size and how you use music. Our guide to [PRS and PPL music licensing for pubs](/licensees-guide/prs-ppl-music-licensing-pubs) walks through the detail.

## The money: what quiz night actually earns

Quiz night does not make money on entry fees. It makes money on a quiet Tuesday becoming a busy one.

Here is a worked example. Use your own numbers.

Say ten teams of five, at £2 a head. That is £100 through the door. Prizes are a £40 bar tab for first, £20 for second and a £10 tab for the wooden spoon, so £70 of face value, which is about £58 once the VAT comes off, costing you roughly £20 in stock at a 65% wet gross profit. Question sheets and printing, call it £5. If you pay a host, add £50.

On those numbers the entry money broadly covers the running of the night. The return is the fifty people in your pub who would not otherwise be there. If they spend £12 each across two and a half hours, that is £600 through the till, which is £500 once you take the VAT off, and at 65% wet gross profit that is around £325 of gross profit from a night that used to earn nothing. Work your own version on the net figure, never on the till total, or you will flatter every quiz you ever run.

So the levers that matter are the halftime break and the number of teams, not the entry fee. A well-announced fifteen minute break, with the bar staffed for it, beats any pricing tweak.

## Where the questions come from

Do not use free question packs from the internet. Your teams have Google, and the pub down the road has the same packs.

Three workable options. Write your own, budgeting roughly ninety minutes a week and keeping every round in a spreadsheet so you can reuse it in eighteen months. Buy from a paid question service, usually a few pounds a week. Or hire a host who brings their own material, which costs more per night but removes the job entirely.

Whichever you choose, keep one complete spare round printed and in the drawer. Sheets go missing, projectors fail, and a spare round has rescued more quiz nights than any amount of planning.

## Hosting tips that matter

- **Ninety to 120 minutes of actual quizzing**, which is around two and a half hours door to door with the break and the marking.
- **Read every question twice**, slowly, and never take a third request from one table.
- **Swap sheets between teams for marking.** It is faster, it is fairer, and it stops arguments landing on you.
- **Use a microphone** if you have more than about eight teams. Shouting for two hours ruins the pace and your voice.
- **Set the phone rule once, at the start,** and be light about it. Public shaming kills the mood.
- **Keep the rules identical every week.** Regulars want a familiar structure, not your latest idea.
- **Start on time.** If you say 8pm and start at 8.20, you have taught everyone to arrive at 8.20 next week.

## How to fill the room in the first six weeks

A new quiz is quiet for a month. Plan for it rather than panicking at week two.

Tell your existing regulars personally, by name, three weeks out. Put a chalkboard by the door and another by the bar. Create one Facebook event and post to it weekly, not once. Get every team that turns up to give you a team name and a WhatsApp or email contact, then send one short reminder on the morning of the quiz. That contact list becomes the most valuable thing the quiz produces.

Make it bookable too. At The Anchor we grew table bookings by 403%, and a big part of that was letting people reserve a table for an event instead of hoping there would be room. A team that has booked turns up.

## Common mistakes

- Overly long quizzes. Anything past two hours of questions and people leave before the results.
- Changing the format every week, so nobody knows what they are turning up to.
- Prizes big enough to attract quiz specialists who buy one round of drinks and leave.
- No break, or a break nobody was told about, so the bar takes nothing.
- Running it on a Friday, competing with your own best trading night.

## Quick checklist

- Four round types chosen and one interactive round scheduled.
- Monthly theme in the diary.
- Leaderboard visible and updated in public.
- Fifteen minute break announced and the bar staffed for it.
- Music licence checked if you run audio rounds.
- Tiebreak is a question, never a draw.
- Team contact list collected from week one, spare round printed.

## Mini FAQ

**How long before I know if it is working?**
Six weeks, minimum, run on the same night at the same time. Judging a new quiz after two weeks is how good quizzes get cancelled.
