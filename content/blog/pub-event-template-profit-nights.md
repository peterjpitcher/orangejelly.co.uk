# The Ultimate Pub Event Template for Guaranteed Profit Nights

Events only work when they are structured, and [running a pub event well](/guides/how-to-run-successful-pub-events) starts long before the doors open. This template turns any activation into a repeatable profit night by locking in pricing, staffing, marketing, and post-event follow up.

The reason most pub events lose money is not the idea. It is that nobody decided the capacity, nobody priced against actual costs, and the promotion was one Facebook post six days out. All three of those are fixable with a run sheet.

This is the standard process I run at The Anchor for any event, from a quiz to a beer festival. The dates change. The structure does not.

## The four numbers that decide whether it makes money

Before you pick a theme, write down four figures. Everything else in this template exists to serve them.

1. **Realistic capacity.** Not your fire capacity, the number you can genuinely sell and genuinely serve. If your kitchen can plate 40 covers in an hour, 60 ticket holders arriving at 7pm is a bad night, not a busy one.
2. **Ticket yield.** What each ticket contributes after the goods it entitles the holder to, and after VAT if you are registered.
3. **Incremental spend per head.** What guests spend at the bar on top of the ticket.
4. **Incremental cost.** Entertainment, extra labour, prizes, print. Only what you would not have spent on a normal night.

Judge the event against a normal trading night of the same weekday. A Tuesday event that takes £900 is only worth running if a normal Tuesday does not.

## Six-week timeline

**Week 6 to 4: confirm the concept**

- Choose one clear theme from your [pub event ideas](/guides/pub-event-ideas) shortlist and commit. No hybrid themes.
- Set capacity, start time, finish time and price.
- Book the act or host in writing, with the fee, the set times and a cancellation clause.
- Clear the paperwork (see the next section) while there is still time to fix a problem.
- Build the booking page or the booking sheet. If it is not bookable, it is not launched.

**Week 4 to 2: launch and book**

- Open bookings with a deposit and announce across every channel on the same day.
- Confirm suppliers, order the extra stock and pencil the staff rota.
- Take the first ten bookings personally, by text, from your regulars. Momentum sells the rest.

**Week 2 to 0: execute and sell**

- Run the content push (grid below) and post remaining availability twice a week.
- Brief the team with the run sheet and the upsell lines.
- Lock the final headcount 48 hours out and confirm every booking by text.

Only three weeks to work with? Cut the middle block, not the promotion. Concept and price on day one, bookings open on day two, then promote every second day until the night.

## Clear the paperwork in week six

Most events need nothing beyond your existing premises licence. Some do, and finding out five days beforehand is how a night dies.

You need a Temporary Event Notice if the event goes past your licensed hours, or puts licensable activity somewhere outside your licensed footprint, such as a bar in the car park. A standard notice must reach the council at least ten clear working days before the event and costs £21. A late notice is possible between five and nine clear working days out, but the police or environmental health can block it with no right of appeal. A single premises can use up to 15 notices covering a maximum of 21 days a year, and consecutive events need a 24-hour gap. Our [premises and personal licence guide](/guides/pub-licensing-premises-personal-licence-guide) covers where the boundaries sit.

Music needs its own permission. Live or recorded, public performance requires a licence from PPL PRS, priced per event and banded by audience size for one-off events. If you already hold TheMusicLicence, check the event is inside your existing cover before assuming it is, and read our guide to [PRS and PPL music licensing](/guides/prs-ppl-music-licensing-pubs) if you are starting from scratch.

For external acts, ask for their public liability insurance certificate and confirm their electrical equipment has been tested. Write a one-page risk assessment for anything involving fire, height, crowds or a moved bar. It takes twenty minutes and it is the first thing anyone asks for when something goes wrong.

## Pricing formula

Use a simple formula to protect margin:

`(cost of goods + labour uplift + overhead buffer) / target GP = ticket price`

The bit most licensees miss is deciding, up front, whether the ticket **carries** the event or merely **underwrites** it.

**Ticket carries the event.** Tastings, supper clubs, anything where the ticket is the product and the bar uplift is small. Say a six-wine tasting for 24 people: £9.20 of wine per head, £2.80 of cheese and bread, plus a £150 host fee, which is £6.25 a head across 24. That is £18.25 of direct cost. At a 60% target GP the net price is £45.63, or £54.75 with VAT. Round it to £55. If that price will not sell in your town, cut the specification, not the margin.

**Ticket underwrites the event.** Quizzes, live music, sport, anything with a strong bar uplift. Here the ticket only has to cover direct costs so the night cannot lose money, and the profit arrives over the bar. A pie-and-quiz night for 60: £3.90 food cost per head, plus £300 of fixed costs (£120 quizmaster, roughly £135 of extra labour once you allow for employer National Insurance and holiday pay, £45 of prizes and print) which is £5 a head. Break-even is £8.90 net, about £10.70 including VAT. Sell it at £15 and you are £3.60 a head ahead before anyone orders a drink.

Add a small buffer for no-shows or late changes, and sell to 90% of capacity in your plan so a couple of drop-outs do not break the maths.

## Deposits that stand up

A deposit is one of the biggest levers on attendance, and it is also where pubs write terms that would not survive a challenge.

Under consumer protection law your terms have to be transparent, agreed before the booking is made, and proportionate to the loss you actually suffer. The Competition and Markets Authority's position is that a charge should not leave you better off than if the booking had gone ahead. So do not write "all deposits non-refundable in all circumstances" on a chalkboard and hope.

What works: a modest deposit per head, clearly shown at the point of booking, deducted from the final bill on the night, refundable up to a stated cut-off such as 48 hours. Guests read that as a booking fee rather than a penalty, and it still does the job of committing them.

## Staffing and ops

- Assign three named roles: a host who owns the room and the microphone, a bar lead who owns speed of service, and a floor lead who owns food and clearing.
- Write a run sheet with clock times, not a to-do list. 17:30 tables set. 18:00 team briefing. 18:30 doors. 19:15 kitchen call. 20:00 round one. 21:15 food service. 22:30 last orders and the next-event announcement.
- Pre-batch everything that can be prepped: garnishes portioned, jugs of house cocktail made ahead, glassware brought up before doors.
- Roster one more person than you think you need for the first hour. Arrival is the crunch, not the middle.

## Promotion stack

One announcement is not a campaign. Work the grid.

| When | What goes out |
|------|----------------|
| Week 4 | Launch post with the booking link, plus a text or email to everyone who came to the last one |
| Week 3 | Behind-the-scenes content: the menu, the act, the prizes |
| Week 2 | Availability post ("14 seats left"), plus a poster on the door and a table tent on every table |
| Week 1 | Two stories with live availability, one reminder to bookers, one push in local community groups |
| Day of | Morning story, afternoon reminder text to bookers, chalkboard out front |

Ask your team to mention the event to every seated guest for the fortnight beforehand. That single habit outsells any paid post.

## On-the-night playbook

- Greet and explain the flow within the first minute, so nobody spends the night unsure where the food is.
- Collect content early, while the room looks full and people look fresh. Nothing usable gets shot at 10:45pm.
- Upsell one add-on and one only (dessert, a premium pour, or a raffle ticket) using your [upselling scripts](/guides/upselling-secrets-training-scripts). Ask everyone, once, at the same moment.
- Announce the next event from the microphone before people leave, and open bookings for it that night.

## Post-event: prove it made money

Do this the next morning, while the till report is fresh.

Send a thank-you message within 24 hours, ask your happiest guests for a review, and offer bookers priority access to the next date.

Then run the arithmetic. Take the quiz night above: 60 tickets at £15 gives £900 gross, £750 net of VAT, less £234 of food cost, which is £516. Take off £300 of fixed event costs and the ticket line contributes £216. If those 60 guests buy an average of two and a half drinks at £5.20, that is £780 gross, £650 net, and at a 68% wet GP that is £442 of gross profit. Total contribution about £658. If a normal Tuesday contributes £150, the event earned you roughly £500 it would not otherwise have had, plus 60 names for the database. That is the number that tells you whether to run it again.

## Common mistakes

- No capacity cap, so the kitchen collapses and the review says "slow service".
- Pricing on what feels right instead of on direct cost and target GP.
- Forgetting to promote after the first announcement.
- Discovering in week one that the notice or music licence was needed in week six.
- Never comparing the night against a normal trading session of the same weekday.

## Quick checklist

- Capacity and price set, based on the four numbers.
- Licensing and music permissions confirmed.
- Booking link live with a deposit and a clear, fair cancellation cut-off.
- Roles assigned and the timed run sheet printed.
- Promotion grid scheduled for all four weeks.
- Post-event follow up and the profit comparison diarised.

## Mini FAQ

**Do I need a big event every month?**
No. One strong, repeatable event can outperform a busy calendar, and it gets cheaper to run each time because the template is already built.

**Should I ticket or take walk-ins?**
Ticketing gives you predictability and cash. Walk-ins can be a bonus if you hold back a few tables.

**What if it does not sell?**
Cut the capacity rather than the price, tell bookers the room is now intimate rather than empty, and work out whether the problem was the concept or the promotion before you blame the idea.
