## 1. The position in one page

1. **LAW.** Every food business must be able to tell a guest which of the 14 listed allergens are in each non-prepacked food and drink it supplies. This covers plated meals, buffets, canapés, bar drinks and free food.
2. **LAW.** The information can be given "by any means", including by word of mouth. If it is given orally there must be a written signpost where the guest chooses the food, telling them to ask staff.
3. **LAW.** The information must be accurate. That is where most prosecutions start: staff said "no" and the answer was wrong.
4. **LAW.** Written allergen information for loose food is still **not** a legal requirement anywhere in the UK on 13 October 2026. "Owen's Law" has not been enacted.
5. **GUIDANCE.** The FSA (England, Wales, Northern Ireland) recommends written information backed by a conversation. Food Standards Scotland consulted on similar guidance in 2026.
6. **LAW.** Orders taken without the customer present (phone, email, online) fall under the distance selling rule: information before the order is concluded and again at delivery.
7. **GUIDANCE.** For pre-ordered group meals the FSA says allergens "should also be discussed with individuals on the day".
8. **LAW.** Food packed before the guest orders or picks it, on the same premises, is "prepacked for direct sale" and needs a full ingredients label.
9. **LAW.** Penalties differ by nation. In England and Wales a Food Information Regulations offence carries an unlimited fine; in Scotland the cap for that offence is £5,000, and the Northern Ireland statute points to the same figure (conflict with FSA guidance flagged at 11.3). Unsafe food offences carry heavier penalties everywhere, including prison.
10. **Cases.** Recent convictions include a Hertfordshire pub (a birthday meal), a Belfast restaurant (a private event) and a London cafe where a child died. A Dorset inquest concerned a family pub lunch pre-ordered by email.

## 3. Topic 1: the legal duty for loose food at events

**1.1 Allergen information is mandatory for food sold without prepackaging, food packed at the customer's request and food prepacked for direct sale.**
- LAW. VERIFIED (primary). [L5] FIC Article 44(1)(a), read with Article 9(1)(c).
- Quote: "the provision of the particulars specified in point (c) of Article 9(1) is mandatory;" (Article 44(1)(a)).
- Nations: the assimilated FIC applies in England, Wales and Scotland. In Northern Ireland the EU regulation itself applies. [G1] para 1: "largely contained in assimilated Regulation (EU) No. 1169/2011 (for England and Wales) and Regulation (EU) No. 1169/2011 (for Northern Ireland)". The duty is the same in all four nations.

**1.2 What must be given: which of the 14 listed substances were used as an ingredient or processing aid and are still in the finished food. A full ingredients list is not required for loose food.**
- LAW. VERIFIED (primary). [L5] Article 9(1)(c): "used in the manufacture or preparation of a food and still present in the finished product, even if in an altered form".
- GUIDANCE on the law. [G1] para 78: "The provision does not require a food business to provide a full ingredients list, with the exception of prepacked for direct sale food".
- [G1] para 84: "Allergen information must be made available for the entire dish as served".

**1.3 It can be given by any means, including orally.**
- LAW. VERIFIED (primary). England [L1] reg 5(1): "by any means the operator chooses, including, subject to paragraph (3), orally". Wales [L2] reg 5(1) and Northern Ireland [L4] reg 5(1) use the same words. Scotland [L3] reg 5(1): "in any manner that they choose, including, subject to paragraph (3), orally."
- GUIDANCE. [G1] para 80: "FBOs have flexibility to provide allergen information for non-prepacked food by any means, including orally by a member of staff."

**1.4 If it is given orally, the business must display a written signpost telling customers to ask staff.**
- LAW. VERIFIED (primary). England [L1] reg 5(3): "the operator must indicate that details of that substance or product can be obtained by asking a member of staff." Northern Ireland [L4] reg 5(3) is identical; Wales [L2] reg 5(3) and Scotland [L3] reg 5(3) are to the same effect.
- Where it must appear, England [L1] reg 5(4): on a label attached to the food, or "on a notice, menu, ticket or label that is readily discernible by an intending purchaser at the place where the intending purchaser chooses that food." Scotland [L3] reg 5(4)(b): "on a notice, menu, ticket or label that is readily discernible by an intending purchaser at the place where they choose that food."
- GUIDANCE. [G1] para 81: the business "must use clear signposting to direct the customer to where this information can be found". [G11]: "a sign must be displayed informing the customers where the information can be found".
- Event point (GOOD PRACTICE, drawn from the wording of reg 5(4)): the signpost has to be where the guest chooses the food. At an event that may be the pre-order form, the menu card, the buffet table and the bar, not only a poster by the front door.

**1.5 Where an ingredient is derived from a listed allergen, the information must name the allergen clearly.**
- LAW. VERIFIED (primary). [L1] reg 5(5): the particulars "must be made available with a clear reference to the name of the substance or product listed in Annex II". Breach of reg 5(5) is itself an offence in England, Wales and Northern Ireland (see 11.2).

**1.6 The information must be available, easy to get at and accurate.**
- LAW. VERIFIED (primary). [L5] Article 12(1): "Mandatory food information shall be available and shall be easily accessible, in accordance with this Regulation, for all foods." Article 7(2): "Food information shall be accurate, clear and easy to understand for the consumer."
- GUIDANCE. [G1] para 17: "All information provided about allergens must be accurate, however it is provided." [G1] para 87: "Whatever the chosen method of presentation, the FBO must always ensure that the allergen information is current and accurate." [G3] para 57: "The allergen information provided to consumers must be accurate. This is a legal requirement as well as being vital to ensure the safety of consumers."

**1.7 Must oral information be "consistent and verifiable"? The legislation does not use those words. The guidance does, and the wording differs by nation.**
- LAW. VERIFIED (primary) by reading reg 5 in all four nations and FIC Articles 7, 9, 12, 14 and 44: neither "consistent" nor "verifiable" appears. The legal tests are that the information is given, is easily accessible and is accurate.
- GUIDANCE, England, Wales, Northern Ireland. [G1] para 80: "No matter how information regarding allergenic ingredients is provided, it must be easily accessible and accurate." [G1] para 82: "To ensure that oral information is accurate, allergen information can be contained on a chart, in a recipe book or on ingredient information sheets, which staff can easily refer to."
- GUIDANCE, FSA food safety pack. [G11]: "Allergen information must be accurate, consistent and up-to date." and "There should be a way to check that the information provided is correct and complete."
- GUIDANCE, Scotland. [F1] para 83: "To ensure that oral information is verifiable, ingredients information can be contained on a chart, in a recipe book or on ingredients information sheets, which staff can easily refer to." [F1] para 82: businesses giving information orally must ensure "the information provided orally by staff members is accurate and reviewed regularly". [F6] module 6: "A process must be in place to ensure that allergen information can be easily obtained and is accurate and consistent."
- Local authority view. [W1]: written back-up to a conversation "would ensure that it is accurate and consistent."
- Practical reading: an inspector will expect staff answers to be backed by a written record (matrix, recipe sheets, labels). That expectation comes from guidance, not from the words of the regulation.

**1.8 The duty covers drinks and free food.**
- GUIDANCE on the law. [G2]: food businesses must "provide allergen information to the consumer for both prepacked and non-prepacked food and drink". [G3] para 3: "This applies to all food supplied by food businesses, including when food is offered complimentarily or otherwise without charge."
- LAW behind it. VERIFIED (primary). [L8] Article 3(8) defines placing on the market as "including offering for sale or any other form of transfer, whether free of charge or not". [L9] Food Safety Act 1990 s 2(1)(a) treats "the supply of food, otherwise than on sale, in the course of a business;" as a sale. [L5] Article 1(3): "It shall apply to all foods intended for the final consumer, including foods delivered by mass caterers, and foods intended for supply to mass caterers."

**1.9 Regulation 5 does not cover food ordered at a distance. A separate rule applies (see Topic 4).**
- LAW. VERIFIED (primary). [L1] reg 5(2) applies to food "offered for sale to a final consumer or to a mass caterer otherwise than by means of distance communication".

## 4. Topic 2: the FSA best practice guidance and the state of reform

**2.1 The FSA recommends written allergen information supported by a conversation.**
- GUIDANCE. VERIFIED (primary). [G3] para 28: "It is best practice that food businesses should make allergen information easily available in writing for consumers and ensure staff are able to support this with a conversation." [G2]: "Our advice to food businesses providing non-prepacked food is that written allergen information, supported by a conversation, works best for consumers." Also in [G1], best practice box under para 83: "Food businesses should make allergen information easily available in writing for consumers and ensure staff can support this with a conversation".
- Applies to England, Wales and Northern Ireland (stated on the GOV.UK page). It does not apply in Scotland.
- Publication date: GOV.UK records 24 February 2025. The FSA's own research paper says: "On 5th March 2025, the FSA published new best practice guidance on the provision of allergen information for non-prepacked foods" [B8]. Cite it as "2025" or give both dates.

**2.2 It is not law.**
- GUIDANCE (statement of legal status). VERIFIED (primary). [G3] para 17: "You are not required by law to follow best practice guidance." It continues: "While you are not required by law to follow best practice guidance, an approach consistent with this guidance should ensure you meet the relevant responsibilities and consumer expectations."
- [G3] para 23: "The minimum legislative requirement for information to be provided by any means (with a sign indicating when it will be provided verbally) has not changed."

**2.3 What it recommends in practice (all GUIDANCE, VERIFIED (primary), [G3]).**
- Where: "Written allergen information should be readily available, if possible, without consumers having to ask for it" (para 37), for example on the main menu, in a booklet on the counter or on a wall matrix.
- Four principles for the written information: easy to use, clear, comprehensive (all 14) and accurate (para 29, paraphrased).
- Digital formats need a fallback: "they should have an alternative way of accessing the information for those who may not be able to access the information digitally" (para 45).
- Ask everyone: "Food businesses should ask all consumers whether they have any allergen requirements to encourage these discussions." (para 66).
- Decide who handles it: "Food businesses should decide who is best placed to have allergen conversations." (para 63).
- No guessing: "It is important that staff do not guess or make assumptions based on what ingredients they think may or may not be present." (para 55).
- Pass it to the kitchen in writing: "the information should be passed directly to the person preparing the food in writing and there should be confirmation that they have received and understood the information" (para 79).
- Confirm at the table: "The server should also verbally confirm the food meets the allergen requirements" (para 81).
- Other allergens: "Food businesses should be able to tell a consumer if other ingredients are present in food if asked where possible." (para 55).

1. celery
2. cereals containing gluten (such as wheat, rye, barley and oats)
3. crustaceans (such as prawns, crabs and lobsters)
4. eggs
5. fish
6. lupin
7. milk
8. molluscs (such as mussels and oysters)
9. mustard
10. peanuts
11. sesame

## 6. Topic 4: pre-ordered food and distance selling

**4.1 For loose food offered for sale at a distance, allergen information must be available before the purchase is concluded and at the moment of delivery, at no extra charge.**
- LAW. VERIFIED (primary). [L5] Article 14(2): "In the case of non-prepacked foods offered for sale by means of distance communication, the particulars required under Article 44 shall be made available in accordance with paragraph 1 of this Article." Article 14(1)(a): information "shall be available before the purchase is concluded" and provided "without the food business operator charging consumers supplementary costs". Article 14(1)(b): "all mandatory particulars shall be available at the moment of delivery."
- Nations: same rule everywhere. Enforcement differs (11.2): in Scotland a breach of Article 14 is a direct offence [L3] Schedule 3; in England it is on the improvement notice list [L1] Schedule 5.

**4.2 What "distance" means.**
- LAW. VERIFIED (primary). [L5] Article 2(2)(u): "any means which, without the simultaneous physical presence of the supplier and the consumer, may be used for the conclusion of a contract between those parties."
- GUIDANCE. [G1] footnote: "Electronic order screens in store and drive through order screens are not ‘means of distance communication’ because of the simultaneous physical presence of the supplier and the consumer."

**4.3 What the FSA says about phone and online orders.**
- GUIDANCE on the law. VERIFIED (primary). [G2]: "If food is sold online or by phone through distance selling, allergen information must be provided at two stages in the order process." The page says the first stage can be in writing (website, catalogue or menu) or orally by phone, and the second in writing (stickers, enclosed menu) or orally by phone.
- [G1] para 86: businesses "must ensure that mandatory allergen information is available to the consumer (for free)". Para 88: "The allergen information must be provided without any supplementary costs being charged to the customer by the FBO".
- [G11]: businesses taking phone or web orders must be able to tell customers the allergens "before the order is placed AND at the point of delivery to the customer".
- GUIDANCE (best practice). [G2]: "you should make written allergen information available to the consumer both before the food is ordered and when it is delivered". [G3] para 48: "Food businesses should also ask consumers whether allergen information is required before taking the order." Para 49: "Where customisations have been made, this should also be indicated."
- Scotland. [F1] para 102: businesses "must ensure that mandatory allergen information is available to the consumer on request (for free)". Note the words "on request", which the FSA text does not use.

**4.4 Does a party pre-order count as distance selling? No regulator statement answers this directly.**
- UNVERIFIED as a classification. The FSA's best practice guidance has a section headed "Distance selling and pre-ordering" but does not say that a pre-order is distance selling.
- What can be said from the law (VERIFIED (primary)): the test is whether the contract is concluded without supplier and customer being present together [L5] Article 2(2)(u). A pre-order form returned by email, an online menu selection or a phone order with a deposit meets those words on their face. A booking made remotely with food chosen and paid for in person on the day would fall under reg 5 instead.
- Safe course (GOOD PRACTICE, consistent with both routes): put the allergen information in writing with the pre-order form or menu, and give it again on the day.
- I found no source that treats deposits differently. The legal trigger is when "the purchase is concluded".

**4.5 What the FSA says about pre-ordered group meals.**
- GUIDANCE. VERIFIED (primary). [G3] para 50: "allergen information should also be discussed with individuals on the day, before the food is served." and "It may not be sufficient to rely only on information supplied in advance as ingredients could have changed or details been missed in the original order."
- GUIDANCE on the law, events. [G4]: "If the food has been ordered before an event by a person or organisation on behalf of other individuals, then it is non-prepacked food." The caterer "should provide mandatory allergen information to anyone consuming the food and make this available orally or in writing". Also: "If a specific food has been ordered in advance, it is not PPDS. You still need to provide mandatory allergen information in some form to the consumer."
- Scotland, training. [F6] module 6: "At time of booking: ask if any members of the party have any allergies".

**4.6 Cases that show the risk** (details in section 8): a family pub lunch pre-ordered by email with no allergy notified (Ferndown, inquest 2023); a phone order with a "nut-free" assurance (Stoke-on-Trent, convicted 2025); an online order (Derby, convicted 2025); an app order from a menu with no allergen information (Newcastle, inquest 2024).

## 7. Topic 5: buffets

**5.1 Allergen information is needed for each buffet item, not for the buffet as a whole.**
- GUIDANCE on the law. VERIFIED (primary). [G2]: "If you offer food in the form of a buffet, you need to provide allergen information for each food item separately. You should not provide it for the buffet as a whole."
- The verbs differ between documents, so quote the one you rely on. FSA technical guidance [G1] para 85: "Where food is provided through a buffet format, the allergen information should be provided for each food item separately." Scottish technical guidance [F1] para 77: "Where food is provided through a buffet format, the allergen information must be provided for each food item separately."
- The underlying LAW is 1.1 and 1.2: the duty attaches to each food.

**5.2 How to do it.**
- GUIDANCE. [G2]: "by labelling the allergens contained in individual dishes, or by displaying a sign directing customers to ask staff for allergen information". The FSA adds that the information must be visible, clearly legible and easily accessible.
- Scotland, training. [F6] module 6: where customers help themselves and cannot talk to staff, "each of the foods in the buffet should be labelled to show any allergenic ingredients".

**5.3 Cross-contamination from shared serving utensils and self-service.**
- I found no FSA (England, Wales, Northern Ireland) text that deals specifically with shared serving utensils on a self-service buffet. The FSA's general statements are: [G6] cross-contact happens through "food to equipment/utensils/surface to food contact" (sharing utensils without washing) and [G2]: "If you can’t avoid cross-contamination in food preparation, you should inform customers that you can’t provide an allergen-free dish."
- Scotland has specific wording. GUIDANCE. VERIFIED (primary). [F6] module 5: "always lay out dishes in a way that will minimise the risk of allergen-free food being contaminated with ingredients from another dish and provide dedicated allergen serving utensils".
- GOOD PRACTICE that follows: plate allergy guests' food from the kitchen rather than from the buffet, and say so on the buffet signage. This is my inference from the sources above, not a quoted requirement.

**5.4 Packaged buffet items.** See Topic 6: individually wrapped items are PPDS; shared platters are not.

## 8. Topic 6: prepacked for direct sale (PPDS, "Natasha's Law")

**6.1 Definition.**
- LAW. VERIFIED (primary). The FIC excludes PPDS food from "prepacked food" without defining it: [L5] Article 2(2)(e): "‘prepacked food’ does not cover foods packed on the sales premises at the consumer’s request or prepacked for direct sale;". The English regulations refer back to that article ([L1] reg 2); the other nations' definitions were not read.
- GUIDANCE on the law. [G4]: "PPDS food is food that is packaged at the same place it is offered or sold to consumers." [G1] para 97: "Any food that is packed on the premises by the same food business in anticipation of an order, before being offered for sale, would be considered to be PPDS food."
- Temporary and outside sites. [G1] para 94 includes food packed elsewhere by the same business and offered "on other premises if the food is offered for sale from a moveable and/or temporary premises (such as marquees, market stalls, mobile sales vehicles)".
- Who it covers. [G4]: "this applies to any food business that produces PPDS food including event caterers, hotels, bars and pubs".

**6.2 What the label must show.**
- LAW. VERIFIED (primary). England [L1] reg 5A(1): the business "must provide directly on the package or on a label attached to the package the particulars required by" Article 9(1)(b) (ingredients list) and Article 9(1)(c) (allergens, emphasised as Article 21 requires). Wales [L2], Scotland [L3] and Northern Ireland [L4] each have a reg 5A. The amendment notes on legislation.gov.uk show 1 October 2021 as the start date in all four nations.
- GUIDANCE. [G1] para 16: "These changes place a duty on food businesses to label PPDS food with the name of the food and a full list of ingredients containing emphasised allergens." [G4]: "Any food business that produces PPDS food will be required to label it with the name of the food and a full ingredients list."
- Handwritten labels are allowed if they meet the legal font requirements [G4] (paraphrased).
- Scotland: FSS confirms the same rule has applied in Scotland and the rest of the UK since 1 October 2021 [F7] (paraphrased).

**6.3 When event food becomes PPDS** (GUIDANCE on the law, VERIFIED (primary), [G4] unless stated).
- Individually packed items made before guests choose: the FSA lists packaged sandwiches, cakes, bread, pies and pasties, packaged salads and soup already in pots.
- Packed lunches or boxes put out for guests to pick on the day: "If an individual has given an indication of their dietary requirement in advance but has not ordered a specific food in advance and is presented with food options that are in packaging on the day, this is PPDS food."
- Items wrapped in cling film individually are PPDS if the wrap meets the packaging definition (FAQ, paraphrased).
- Drinks: "But if you pour and lid drinks before consumers order them, in anticipation of a rush, the drinks would be PPDS and would need labelling."
- Eating in or taking away makes no difference: "If the food is PPDS, labelling rules apply whether the consumer consumes it on premises or at another location." [G5].

**6.4 What is not PPDS** (GUIDANCE on the law, VERIFIED (primary)).
- Packed at the customer's request: "Food placed into packaging at the consumer’s request is not PPDS." [G4]. [G1] para 98 gives a sandwich or burger wrapped after the order as an example.
- Specific food ordered in advance (see 4.5).
- Shared platters: "So individually packaged sandwiches would be PPDS but not one large platter of sandwiches intended for different consumers attending a buffet." [G4].
- Cling film taken off before service: "No, if the clingfilm is removed before the food is presented to the consumer then it is not caught by PPDS labelling requirements." [G4].
- A whole cake under a dome that is sliced and plated is not PPDS [G4] (FAQ, paraphrased).
- Loose items such as "unpackaged complimentary bread placed on tables" [G5].
- Distance sales: "If the food is sold by distance selling, such as over the phone and internet then the new PPDS rules do not apply." [G4]. [G1] para 104: "The requirement for a list of ingredients does not apply to PPDS food sold online, via telephone or provided to the consumer by mail order only." The Article 14 allergen rule still applies.
- Food packed by another business (a bought-in wrapped cake, for example) is ordinary prepacked food and needs the supplier's full label: "PPDS food does not cover food packed by one business and supplied to another business for sale" [G1] para 100.
- In every one of these cases the allergen information duty for loose food still applies.

**6.5 A wrinkle in the FSA event caterers page.** The page lists "enclosed food platters" among PPDS examples, but also says PPDS "does not include food packaged in larger quantities (such as platters of sandwiches covered in clingfilm)." [G4]. The FAQ quoted at 6.4 resolves it: the test is whether the item is a single packaged item presented to one consumer.

**6.6 Wedding favours, sweet bags and take-home boxes.** The FSA pages do not mention these by name (UNVERIFIED for those specific items). Applying the FSA's test: packed to a specific advance order from the organiser, they are non-prepacked ("if the company who orders the food for an event is not itself a food business, it can be considered to be the ‘final consumer’" [G4]); packed speculatively by the venue and offered to guests to choose, they are PPDS. Either way allergen information must be available. Check borderline cases with the local authority.

## 9. Topic 7: "may contain", "free from", "gluten-free" and "vegan"

**7.1 Precautionary allergen labelling (PAL) is voluntary, but must not mislead.**
- GUIDANCE. VERIFIED (primary). [G7]: "There is no specific legal requirement for food businesses to label food with ‘may contain’." [G1] para 25: "FBOs should provide consumers with accurate information about the risk of the unintended presence of allergens".
- LAW behind it. [L5] Article 36(2): voluntary information must not mislead and "it shall not be ambiguous or confusing for the consumer;".

**7.2 Only after a risk assessment.**
- GUIDANCE. VERIFIED (primary). [G6]: "A precautionary allergen label should only be used when, following a risk assessment, a risk of allergen cross-contact within the supply chain is identified that cannot be removed through risk management actions." [G1]: use it only "if an unavoidable risk of allergen cross-contamination has been identified following a risk assessment". [G11] says "may contain" statements "are only used if the risk of allergenic cross contamination is high after a full risk assessment".
- "PAL should not be used as a substitute for good food hygiene and safety practices." [G6].
- Blanket statements such as "may contain allergens" "could be deemed misleading to the consumer" [G6].

**7.3 Never with a "free from" claim for the same allergen.**
- GUIDANCE. VERIFIED (primary). [G6]: "Precautionary allergen labelling should not be used in combination with a free-from statement for the same allergen." [G1] para 67: "Precautionary Allergen Labelling statements should not be used in conjunction with a ‘free from’ statement for the same allergen".

**7.4 For loose food it can be said rather than labelled, and supplier warnings should be passed on.**
- GUIDANCE. VERIFIED (primary). [G6]: "For non-prepacked foods precautionary allergen statements do not need to be included on a label, as information on the unintentional presence of allergens can be communicated by other means." and "Precautionary allergen information from ingredient suppliers should be passed to the final consumer, unless the food business risk assessment supersedes that information."
- [G12]: if an ingredient label says it may contain an allergen, "you need to let the customer know and ask them if they still wish to order".

**7.5 "Free from" is a guarantee.**
- GUIDANCE. VERIFIED (primary). [G2]: "A free-from claim is a guarantee that the food is suitable for all with an allergy or intolerance." [G1] para 68: "Only free-from allergen claims are guarantees that the specified allergen is absent; to use it, a food business must have implemented strict controls to eliminate any risk of cross-contamination."
- [G11]: "You must also make sure that the environment is free from that allergen and that there is no risk of the food coming into contact with that allergen."
- [G3] para 53: where "free from" and "contains" information share a document, "it must be very clear if the dish contains peanuts or is peanut free to avoid confusion".

**7.6 "Gluten-free": the 20 parts per million rule.**
- LAW. VERIFIED (primary). [L6] Regulation (EU) No 828/2014, Annex: "The statement ‘gluten-free’ may only be made where the food as sold to the final consumer contains no more than 20 mg/kg of gluten."
- GUIDANCE on the law. [G1] para 31: the rules "apply to all foods including non-prepacked foods such as those served in restaurants." Para 69: gluten-free statements "are not absolute claims but stipulate that levels of gluten in the food are below 20mg/kg".
- "No gluten containing ingredients" wording: [G1] para 89 says such "statements can be misleading to consumers" and the best practice is to avoid them.
- Kitchen reality. [G2]: where wheat flour is handled and cross-contamination cannot be removed, do not make gluten-free or wheat-free claims (paraphrased).
- Nations: Wales [L2] reg 10(1)(c), Scotland [L3] reg 10(aa) and Northern Ireland [L4] reg 10(1)(b) make breach of Regulation 828/2014 a specific offence. The English regulations do not mention Regulation 828/2014 at all (text check of SI 2014/1855, 13 October 2026). I did not find the English enforcement provision; a false "gluten-free" claim in England would still engage the misleading information and unsafe food offences in Topic 11. UNVERIFIED on the English route.

**7.7 "Vegan" does not mean safe for people with allergies.**
- GUIDANCE. VERIFIED (primary). [G7]: for people allergic to milk, eggs, crustaceans, fish or molluscs, "you should never assume a product labelled as vegan is safe to eat". Also: "the term ‘vegan’ is not defined in food law".
- [G2]: "Customers sometimes assume that vegan meals are free-from animal based allergens (egg, fish, crustaceans, molluscs, milk)." The FSA says this is not always so because of low level cross-contamination.
- A "may contain" statement can sit alongside a vegan label: "Precautionary Allergen Labelling statements can be used in combination with a ‘vegan’ label" [G1] para 68.

**7.8 Proposed change: international PAL thresholds.**
- VERIFIED (primary). In December 2025 "The Board agreed to support Codex’s work, subject to gluten resolution" on a global PAL standard using thresholds [B4] para 7.11; background in [B7]. This is not UK law and changes nothing for venues today.

## 10. Topic 8: cross-contamination controls the regulator expects

**8.1 The legal base.**
- LAW. VERIFIED (primary). [L8] Article 14(1): "Food shall not be placed on the market if it is unsafe." Article 14(4)(c) requires regard "to the particular health sensitivities of a specific category of consumers where the food is intended for that category of consumers."
- [L7] Annex II Chapter IX para 3: "food is to be protected against any contamination likely to render the food unfit for human consumption, injurious to health". Article 5 requires procedures based on HACCP principles. The hygiene regulation is enforced through [L10] in England and the national Food Hygiene Regulations 2006 elsewhere [L20].
- **Northern Ireland only**: [L18] Regulation (EU) 2021/382 adds an allergen-specific rule that equipment used for an allergen must not be used for other food "unless the equipment, conveyances and/or containers have been cleaned and checked at least for the absence of any visible debris of that substance or product." It also adds a "food safety culture" duty. Lord Bethell told the Lords committee: "Food business operators in Northern Ireland will be required to meet the new requirements as set out in the amended Regulation." and "As this amending Regulation came into force after 1 January 2021, it does not apply within the territory of Great Britain" [L19]. The Great Britain text of Annex II on legislation.gov.uk has no such provision (checked 13 October 2026).

**8.2 The food safety packs.** England and Wales use Safer food, better business (SFBB). Northern Ireland has its own: "Food safety management guides for caterers and retailers in Northern Ireland" [G9] (Safe Catering). Scotland uses CookSafe (named on the FSS hospitality page). GUIDANCE.

**8.3 What the SFBB allergen safe methods say** (GUIDANCE, VERIFIED (primary), [G11] and [G12]).
- Know what is in the food: "You need to make sure that you know what is in the food you provide." [G2]. Record allergen ingredients, for example "recorded on product specification sheets" [G2].
- Recipes: [G3] para 59 lists "keeping a record of recipes so they are made to the same specification each time."
- Deliveries and substitutions: "Check deliveries to make sure you have the correct order and labelling information is provided." and "If you receive the wrong order or a different product, there may be different allergens in the food." [G11]. Checklist [G8]: "if there are any changes to the ingredients in your regular order (for example substitutions), do you have a procedure in place to approve, record and report this?" [G3] para 59 recommends "minimising the occasions where last minute substitutions are required (if this occurs, ingredients should be checked and allergen information updated accordingly)" and "conducting checks with suppliers to ensure they provide accurate allergen information and ask them to agree to inform the businesses of any allergen changes".
- Clean first: when asked for a dish without an allergen, "make sure work surfaces and equipment have been thoroughly cleaned first" [G12].
- Separate equipment: "You should also have separate preparation boards and equipment dedicated to allergy-free meals." [G12].
- Mistakes: "do not just remove the ingredient containing the allergen from the dish and still serve the food - start from scratch with fresh ingredients." [G12].
- Cooking does not help: "Remember: unlike bacteria, allergens are always present in the food and cannot be removed or destroyed by cooking." [G12].
- Storage: "storing ingredients and prepared foods separately in closed and labelled containers" [G2].
- Utensils: "cleaning utensils before each usage, especially if they were used to prepare meals containing allergens" [G2].
- Right plate, right person: "During service, it is also important to ensure that the right meal is served to the correct person." [G12].

**8.4 Shared fryers and oil.**
- GUIDANCE. VERIFIED (primary). [G2]: "To cook gluten-free chips, you can’t use the same oil which has been previously used for cooking battered fish". [G6] lists cross-contact "through shared fryers for cooking food containing different allergen content".
- Scotland. [F6] module 5: "do not cook food in oil in which you have cooked other foods" and "The fryer must be emptied, thoroughly cleaned and refilled with fresh oil to prevent contamination."

**8.5 The allergen matrix.**
- GUIDANCE. VERIFIED (primary). [G1] para 82 (quoted at 1.7) names a chart, recipe book or ingredient sheets. The FSA publishes a blank allergen matrix with [G2], and its checklist suggests asking kitchen staff to use a matrix when planning recipes [G8].

**8.6 When cross-contamination cannot be avoided.**
- GUIDANCE. [G2]: "If you can’t avoid cross-contamination in food preparation, you should inform customers that you can’t provide an allergen-free dish."

## 11. Topic 9: third-party food (customers' own food, outside caterers, cake makers)

**9.1 Who is a food business operator.**
- LAW. VERIFIED (primary). [L8] Article 3(3): "the natural or legal persons responsible for ensuring that the requirements of food law are met within the food business under their control;". A food business is any undertaking, "whether for profit or not", carrying out any stage of production, processing or distribution of food (Article 3(2), paraphrased).

**9.2 An outside caterer supplying the guests is a food business and carries the allergen duty for its food.**
- GUIDANCE on the law. VERIFIED (primary). [G4]: "Caterers supplying food should provide allergen information to enable consumers to make safe and informed food choices."
- LAW. [L5] Article 8(1): the operator responsible for food information "shall be the operator under whose name or business name the food is marketed". [G1] para 75: "Every FBO in the food supply chain is responsible for ensuring that the allergen information they provide is accurate."

**9.3 Where a cake maker or caterer supplies the venue and the venue serves or sells the food, information must pass down the chain and the venue must give it to guests.**
- LAW. VERIFIED (primary). [L5] Article 8(6): information on non-prepacked food "shall be transmitted to the food business operator receiving the food". The venue then has the reg 5 duty for food it offers (1.1 to 1.6).
- GUIDANCE. [G12]: "Make sure, at the point of delivery, you label the food and check you have all the ingredient information you need from the supplier."
- A home baker who sells cakes may be considered a food business and need to register: the FSA says registration applies to those who sell, cook, store, handle, prepare or distribute food, including businesses trading from home [G14] (paraphrased).

**9.4 Private individuals who are not a food business.**
- GUIDANCE. VERIFIED (primary). [G10] (updated 4 September 2026, written for community and charity events): "Allergen labelling law applies to registered food businesses" and "If your activity does not need to be registered as a food business, you are not legally required to provide allergen information for foods you supply." The FSA still encourages providing it.
- Applying that to a family bringing home-made food to a wake or a guest's home-made birthday cake is my reading by analogy. The page does not address private functions in licensed premises. UNVERIFIED for that scenario.

**9.5 A customer's own cake or buffet brought into the venue: no authoritative answer found.**
- UNVERIFIED. I found no FSA, FSS, local authority, CIEH, UKHospitality or Primary Authority statement on who holds the allergen duty when a customer brings food and venue staff store, cut or serve it. The search allowance ran out before council, CIEH and UKHospitality sources could be searched.
- What the legislation says (VERIFIED (primary)): the reg 5 duty falls on an operator who "offers for sale" the food [L1] reg 5(1); the Food Safety Act treats supply "in the course of a business" as a sale [L9] s 2(1)(a); the hygiene and unsafe food duties apply to whatever the venue itself handles. How those apply to a customer's own cake has not been settled by anything I could find.
- SECONDARY only. The BBC reported in August 2026: "But there is no legal requirement for restaurants to allow people to take in their own food if they have severe allergies." [N4].
- Do not present any firm rule on this in print. Suggested wording: venues should ask their local authority, and in the meantime keep customers' food separate, avoid handling it more than agreed, and make clear to guests that the venue cannot give allergen information for food it did not supply (GOOD PRACTICE, my suggestion).

## 12. Topic 10: staff training

**10.1 The legal requirement.**
- LAW. VERIFIED (primary). [L7] Annex II Chapter XII para 1: operators must ensure "that food handlers are supervised and instructed and/or trained in food hygiene matters commensurate with their work activity;". The assimilated text applies in Great Britain; the EU text applies in Northern Ireland, which also has the food safety culture duty (8.1).

**10.2 The FSA reads this as including allergens, before staff start.**
- GUIDANCE on the law. VERIFIED (primary). [G2]: "Food businesses must make sure that staff receive training on allergens." [G9]: "You must make sure that any member of staff who handles food is trained in food hygiene and safety, including allergens, before they start work."
- Scotland. [F3]: "Food businesses must make sure that their staff are trained on allergens."

**10.3 What staff are expected to be able to do.**
- GUIDANCE. VERIFIED (primary). [G1] para 79: "Members of staff should receive allergen training, know where to find allergen information, and know how to communicate this to customers." [G3] para 62: "Staff should be sufficiently trained to have a conversation about allergens and know where to find the information they need to be able to give accurate information to the consumer."
- A named person on every shift. [G8]: "is a responsible member of staff available on each shift to manage requests from customers with allergies?"
- Emergency awareness. [G8]: "make your staff aware of what to do if a customer has an allergic reaction during a meal".
- Refresher. [G8] (in its "you could also" list, so GOOD PRACTICE): "provide staff with suitable allergen and food hypersensitivity training on an annual basis".
- Records. [G9]: "It is a good idea to keep a record of any training you or your staff have done".

**10.4 Temporary, agency and event staff.**
- The law covers all "food handlers" (10.1) and the FSA's "before they start work" line covers any member of staff who handles food (10.2). The FSA pages read do not single out temporary or agency staff.
- Scotland does. GUIDANCE. VERIFIED (primary). [F6] module 3: "All staff (including temporary staff and contractors) involved in handling ingredients, equipment, utensils, packaging and products should be aware of food allergens". Module 6: "tell all staff when a customer has an allergy, this includes any change of shift staff".

**10.5 Free training.**
- FSA: Food Allergy, Intolerance and Coeliac Disease Training, six modules with tests and a certificate [G13]. The FSA warns that completion "should not be relied upon as evidence of competence" and that "Responsibility for ensuring compliance with relevant legal requirements remains with food business operators." VERIFIED (primary).
- FSS: free online allergy training and an advanced tool, plus MenuCal for allergen and calorie information [F3].

**10.6 Cases on training** (section 13): staff had completed FSA training weeks before but the kitchen still failed (Uxbridge); staff "had not received specific allergy training" (Stoke-on-Trent); a process existed but was not followed (Barking).

## 13. Topic 11: enforcement, penalties and cases

### Enforcement and penalties

**11.1 Who enforces.** [G1] para 105: "In the UK, authorised food officers at Local Authorities have responsibility for official controls relating to allergen rules." GUIDANCE on the law. VERIFIED (primary).

**11.2 Food Information Regulations offences.**
- LAW. VERIFIED (primary). England [L1] reg 10 makes it an offence to fail to comply with listed provisions, including "Article 44(1)(a), as also read with Article 9(1)(c) and regulation 5." (failing to provide allergen information for loose food), reg 5(5) and reg 5A(1)(b). Wales [L2] and Northern Ireland [L4] are in the same form.
- England, Wales and Northern Ireland: failing to put up the "ask staff" signpost (reg 5(3) and (4)) is not on the direct offence list. It is enforced by improvement notice [L1] reg 12(1)(a)(ii).
- Scotland is wider: [L3] reg 10 makes any "specified FIC provision" an offence and also lists "regulation 5(3), (4) or (5);", so a missing signpost and a distance selling failure are direct offences there.

**11.3 Maximum penalty for a Food Information Regulations offence.**
- LAW. VERIFIED (primary). All four nations, reg 11: "A person guilty of an offence under regulation 10 is liable on summary conviction to a fine not exceeding level 5 on the standard scale." [L1], [L3], [L4] (Wales [L2] is the same).
- England and Wales: level 5 is now unlimited. [L14] s 85(1): "the offence is punishable on summary conviction on or after that day by a fine of any amount." [G1] para 108: "A person convicted of an offence under the FIR will be liable to a potentially unlimited fine."
- Scotland: £5,000. [L15] s 225(2) sets level 5 at £5,000. [F1] para 100: "A person convicted of an allergens offence under the FIR 2014 will be liable to a fine not exceeding level 5 on the standard scale, currently £5,000."
- Northern Ireland: [L16] article 5(2) sets level 5 at £5,000. Note the conflict: the FSA technical guidance, which also applies to Northern Ireland, says "potentially unlimited". The statute is the authority; I could not find an instrument lifting the Northern Ireland cap. Check with a Northern Ireland council before printing a figure.

**11.4 Improvement notices.**
- LAW. VERIFIED (primary). [L1] reg 12 applies the Food Safety Act notice power. [L9] s 10(2): "Any person who fails to comply with an improvement notice shall be guilty of an offence." In England appeals go to the First-tier Tribunal; in Wales to the magistrates' court ([L1] and [L2] Schedule 4).
- GUIDANCE. [G2]: "If you fail to act on advice given by the local authority, an improvement notice may be issued." and "In some cases businesses may also face prosecution."
- Law and guidance differ on the appeal period. [G2] says: "You have 14 days to appeal an improvement notice from the date the notice was issued." The Food Safety Act [L9] s 37(5) refers to "one month from the date on which notice of the decision was served on the person desiring to appeal" or the period in the notice if shorter. I did not check the tribunal rules. The safe statement is that the deadline is on the notice.

**11.5 Food Safety Act 1990, sections 14 and 15 (England, Wales, Scotland).**
- LAW. VERIFIED (primary). [L9] s 14(1): it is an offence to sell to the purchaser's prejudice food "which is not of the nature or substance or quality demanded by the purchaser shall be guilty of an offence." (This is the usual charge when a customer asked for "no nuts" and got nuts.) Section 15 covers labels, advertisements or presentation that falsely describe food or are "likely to mislead as to the nature or substance or quality of the food".
- Penalty [L9] s 35(2): "on conviction on indictment, to a fine or to imprisonment for a term not exceeding two years or to both;". On summary conviction: England and Wales, a fine (unlimited) or up to six months; Scotland, a fine up to £20,000 for section 14 or the statutory maximum for section 15, or up to six months.
- Northern Ireland: the equivalent offences are articles 13 and 14 of the Food Safety (Northern Ireland) Order 1991, with penalties in article 36 (up to two years on indictment; £20,000 summary cap for article 13) [L13].
- Defences [L9]: s 21(1) due diligence, that the person "took all reasonable precautions and exercised all due diligence to avoid the commission of the offence by himself or by a person under his control." Section 20: where an offence is due to another person's act or default, "that other person shall be guilty of the offence".

**11.6 Unsafe food (General Food Law, Article 14).**
- LAW. VERIFIED (primary). Offence provisions: England [L10] reg 19; Wales and Scotland [L11] reg 4(b); Northern Ireland [L12] reg 4(b).
- Penalties: England [L10] reg 19(2): "on conviction on indictment to imprisonment for a term not exceeding two years, to a fine or to both." Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland: up to two years on indictment; on summary conviction a fine up to "in the case of an offence under regulation 4(b), £20,000;" or six months [L11] reg 5, [L12] reg 5 (in Wales the summary fine is unlimited under [L14]; see [S2]).
- This is why Scotland's £5,000 cap on labelling offences is not the whole picture. [F1] para 100 says higher penalties are available where Article 14(1) is breached.

**11.7 Sentencing guideline (England and Wales only).**
- VERIFIED (primary). [S1] and [S2]: "Breach of food safety and food hygiene regulations", effective 1 February 2016, last updated 5 October 2026. It covers the England 2013 Regulations reg 19(1), the Food Hygiene (Wales) Regulations 2006 reg 17(1) and the General Food Regulations 2004 reg 4.
- Organisations [S1]: unlimited fine; offence range from a £100 fine to a £3 million fine. Starting points depend on turnover. For a "Micro organisation - Turnover or equivalent: not more than £2 million", very high culpability with harm category 1 starts at £60,000 (range £25,000 to £120,000); low culpability with harm category 3 starts at £200.
- Individuals [S2]: up to two years' custody on indictment; offence range from a conditional discharge to 18 months' custody. It notes: "Regulation 4 of The General Food Regulations 2004 is in force in Wales but not in England."
- The guideline's list of offences does not include the Food Information Regulations or Food Safety Act sections 14 and 15. I did not verify which guideline courts apply to those.
- Scotland and Northern Ireland: no equivalent guideline was found (UNVERIFIED as a negative).

**11.8 Scotland: a duty to tell the regulator.** [F1] para 70 says the Food Safety Act as amended for Scotland means a business "must as soon as reasonably practicable inform Food Standards Scotland if they consider or have reason to believe that food information law is being contravened" in relation to food in its charge. GUIDANCE on the law (the section itself was not read).

## 14. Topic 12: emergency response

**12.1 What the FSA's food safety pack tells caterers to do** (GUIDANCE, VERIFIED (primary), [G12]). If you think a customer is having a severe allergic reaction:
- "do not move them"
- "ring 999 and ask for an ambulance with a paramedic straight away"
- explain that the customer could have anaphylaxis
- "send someone outside to wait for the ambulance"
- "if the customer has an adrenalin or Epi pen, help them to get it."

**12.2 NHS** (VERIFIED (primary), [E1], last reviewed 21 June 2023; its stated review date of 21 June 2026 has passed). Steps include: "Use an adrenaline auto-injector (such as an EpiPen) if you have one", "Call 999 for an ambulance and say that you think you're having an anaphylactic reaction.", lie down (raise legs; sit up slowly if struggling to breathe), use a second injector after 5 minutes if no better, and "Do not stand or walk at any time, even if you feel better."

**12.3 Anaphylaxis UK** (VERIFIED (primary), [E2]). "If the person is conscious, lie them flat with their legs raised to assist in blood flow to the heart and vital organs." Give prescribed adrenaline straight away, call 999 and say "anaphylaxis", give a second dose after five minutes if no improvement, do not let them stand, walk or run, and "Stay with them until emergency services arrive." The page lists a prescription nasal adrenaline as well as auto-injectors: "a needle-free nasal spray".

**12.4 Allergy UK** (VERIFIED (primary), [E3], 7 July 2021). "Call an ambulance (999) and tell the operator it is anaphylaxis"; "lie the person flat with legs raised (or sit them up if having breathing problems)"; "Avoid standing or moving the person having the allergic reaction".

**12.5 Resuscitation Council UK** [E4]: its anaphylaxis guideline is written for healthcare providers. "The most recent version of this Guideline was published in May 2021." I did not find layperson wording on that page.

**12.6 Scotland.** [F6] module 6: "Call the emergency services (999) immediately if you suspect a customer is having an allergic reaction." Send someone to meet the ambulance and stay with the customer.

**12.7 A difference to handle carefully.** The FSA pack says "do not move them". The NHS and both charities say lie the person flat with legs raised (sitting up if breathing is difficult). These are compatible (keep them where they are and get them lying down; never walk them outside), but the wording is not identical. Quote the NHS for positioning.

**12.8 The law on adrenaline.**
- LAW. VERIFIED (primary). Anyone may administer adrenaline to save a life: [L17] reg 238 lifts the restriction on administering a listed prescription medicine "where this is for the purpose of saving life in an emergency." Schedule 19 lists "Adrenaline 1:1000 up to 1mg for intramuscular use in anaphylaxis". Whether that wording reaches the newer nasal spray was not checked.
- Spare auto-injectors: the only exemption for holding stock without a prescription that I found is for schools. [L17] Schedule 17 lists "Persons selling or supplying prescription only medicines to a school." (inhalers and adrenaline auto-injectors). There is no entry for pubs, restaurants or public venues. legislation.gov.uk states Schedule 17 is up to date to 13 October 2026.
- Did the law for hospitality or public venues change in 2025 or 2026? Not on the face of the Regulations. UNVERIFIED whether any consultation or proposal is in progress (search allowance ran out). School allergy rules described as "Benedict's Law" were reported for 2026 (SECONDARY only, [N5]); they do not cover hospitality.

## 15. Topic 13: drinks at events

**13.1 Drinks are covered by the same duty.** [G2] (quoted at 1.8). Draught beer, wine by the glass and cocktails are non-prepacked, so reg 5 applies: any means, with a signpost if oral. [G3] para 5 counts "drinks made to order in a coffee shop" as non-prepacked food. GUIDANCE on the law. VERIFIED (primary).

**13.2 Bottled and canned alcoholic drinks need no ingredients list but must declare allergens.**
- LAW. VERIFIED (primary). [L5] Article 16(4): the ingredients list and nutrition declaration "shall not be mandatory for beverages containing more than 1,2 % by volume of alcohol." Article 21(1): with no ingredients list, the allergen indication "shall comprise the word ‘contains’ followed by the name of the substance or product as listed in Annex II."
- GUIDANCE. [G1] para 70: "a bottle of wine must have a statement such as: ‘Contains: sulphites, milk and egg’ if the finished product contains sulphites at more than 10 mg/litre and if milk or egg residues are detectable in the wine."
- So the bar's source of truth for packaged drinks is the "Contains" statement on the label.

**13.3 Sulphites.** Declarable above 10 mg per litre (3.2). The FSA pack names "wine, beer" among sources [G12] ("Sulphur dioxide (when added and above 10mg/ kg in the finished food and drink)"); FSS names soft drinks, wine and beer [F3]. The sources read do not name cider, so check the label or supplier specification.

**13.4 Gluten cereals in beer.** LAW. VERIFIED (primary). Barley, wheat, rye and oats are Annex II cereals. The only drinks exemption for cereals is "cereals used for making alcoholic distillates including ethyl alcohol of agricultural origin;" [L5] Annex II point 1(d). That covers spirits, not beer. Beer made from these cereals must declare them.

**13.5 Fish finings.** LAW. VERIFIED (primary). Exempt from allergen declaration: "fish gelatine or Isinglass used as fining agent in beer and wine;" [L5] Annex II point 4(b). (This is an allergen exemption only. It says nothing about whether the drink suits vegetarians or vegans.)

**13.6 Milk and egg.** Egg white in a cocktail and cream in a liqueur or cocktail are ordinary ingredients and must be declared. The only milk exemptions are "whey used for making alcoholic distillates including ethyl alcohol of agricultural origin;" and lactitol [L5] Annex II point 7. Milk or egg fining residues in wine are declared if detectable (13.2).

**13.7 Nuts.** LAW. VERIFIED (primary). Nuts are exempt only where they are "nuts used for making alcoholic distillates including ethyl alcohol of agricultural origin;" [L5] Annex II point 8. The sources read do not address liqueurs or gins that are flavoured or infused with nuts after distillation, so I cannot state a rule for those (UNVERIFIED). Use the label and the supplier specification.

**13.8 Pre-poured drinks.** Drinks poured and lidded before the order are PPDS (6.3). Open glasses poured ahead for a drinks reception are not enclosed in packaging, so on my reading of the definition at 6.1 they are non-prepacked (the FSA pages do not give this example); allergen information must still be available.

**13.9 Equipment.** The Mia St Hilaire and Hannah Jacobs cases (section 13) were both drinks: an unwashed blender and the wrong milk. Blenders, shakers, jiggers and milk jugs belong in the allergen controls.

## 16. Topic 14: recording guests' allergy information (data protection)

**14.1 A named guest's allergy is health data, which is special category data.**
- VERIFIED (primary). [I3]: health data "includes any related data which reveals anything about the state of someone’s health". [I1] gives an allergy example: "The hairdresser is therefore processing health data about the client’s allergies."

**14.2 You need two things: a lawful basis and a special category condition.**
- VERIFIED (primary). [I2]: "you must identify both a lawful basis under Article 6 and a condition for processing special category data under Article 9".
- Explicit consent is one condition. [I1]: "explicit consent must be confirmed in a clear statement (whether oral or written), rather than by any other type of affirmative action;". The ICO adds that it must name the type of data and be separate from other consents (paraphrased).
- The ICO's own allergy example uses a different condition (legal claims), for a business recording a test to show it met its duty of care [I1]. The ICO does not say which condition a venue should use for bookings.

**14.3 Keep as little as possible, for as short a time as possible.**
- VERIFIED (primary). [I2]: "it is particularly important to make sure you collect and retain only the minimum amount of special category data". The same page flags security and transparency (privacy information).

**14.4 What the ICO does not say.** I found no ICO guidance aimed at hospitality or event bookings, and no ICO retention period for this data (UNVERIFIED as a negative). Do not print a retention period as an ICO rule.

**14.5 A food safety angle on the same point.** [G3] para 72 notes some customers are uncomfortable disclosing medical information, and "asking if they have “any allergen requirements” is a suitable alternative". The FSA also expects the requirement to be recorded accurately and passed to the kitchen and server (2.3), so some record is expected. GOOD PRACTICE: collect only what the kitchen needs (the allergen and the guest or seat), restrict who sees it, and delete it when the event file is closed.

## 18. Common myths and mistakes that the sources contradict

1. **"Natasha's Law means allergens must be on the menu."** No. It covers PPDS food only. For loose food, [G3] para 23: "The minimum legislative requirement for information to be provided by any means (with a sign indicating when it will be provided verbally) has not changed."
2. **"Owen's Law is already in force" or "comes in during 2026".** Not enacted as at 13 October 2026 (2.5). Several commercial websites say otherwise.
3. **"Natasha's Law started in 2022."** The amendment notes on legislation.gov.uk give 1 October 2021 in all four nations. A BBC report of April 2026 gets this wrong [N1].
4. **"Telling the customer is enough."** Only with the written "ask staff" signpost where they choose the food (1.4), and only if the answer is right (1.6).
5. **"The law says oral information must be consistent and verifiable."** Those words are guidance, not legislation, and Scotland and the FSA use different words (1.7).
6. **"A blanket 'may contain allergens' notice covers us."** [G6]: such statements "could be deemed misleading to the consumer"; PAL needs a risk assessment and is not a substitute for controls (7.2).
7. **"Vegan means safe for milk and egg allergy."** [G7]: "you should never assume a product labelled as vegan is safe to eat".
8. **"Gluten-free means we did not add gluten ingredients."** It means no more than 20 mg/kg in the food as sold (7.6).
9. **"Free food and canapés do not count."** [G3] para 3 says the duty applies "when food is offered complimentarily or otherwise without charge."
10. **"The pre-order form deals with allergies."** [G3] para 50: "It may not be sufficient to rely only on information supplied in advance". The Ferndown inquest is the example [C8].
11. **"One allergen sign for the whole buffet."** [G2]: "You should not provide it for the buffet as a whole."
12. **"A cling-filmed sandwich platter needs an ingredients label."** Not if it is a shared platter or the film comes off before service; individually wrapped items do (6.4).
13. **"Our staff did the FSA course, so we are covered."** The FSA says completion "should not be relied upon as evidence of competence" [G13]. In the Javitri case staff had done the training that month [C9].
14. **"Take the nuts off and send it back out."** [G12]: "start from scratch with fresh ingredients."
15. **"Frying or cooking destroys the allergen."** [G12]: allergens "cannot be removed or destroyed by cooking." Shared oil is a named risk (8.4).
16. **"The maximum fine is £5,000."** True for a Food Information Regulations offence in Scotland and, on the statute, Northern Ireland. In England and Wales it is unlimited, and unsafe food offences carry up to two years' imprisonment everywhere (11.3 to 11.6).
17. **"Only the 14 matter."** They are the only ones the law lists, but the FSA says staff should be able to answer about other ingredients where possible [G3] para 55, and the Anglesey inquest involved an allergen that was never identified [C16].
18. **"Allergens are a food issue, not a bar issue."** Two of the deaths in section 13 were caused by drinks.
19. **"Spirits made from wheat or barley must be declared as gluten."** Distillates are exempt [L5] Annex II point 1(d). Beer is not.
20. **"Beer and wine fined with isinglass must declare fish."** Exempt [L5] Annex II point 4(b).
21. **"Venues must let allergic guests bring their own food."** The BBC reports there is no such legal requirement [N4] (SECONDARY only).
22. **"The matrix was done when we opened."** It must be "current and accurate" [G1] para 87. A bun change produced a conviction in Preston [C11].
